The Willow Tree They Planted Where He Died

Michele and Ray Hornsby lost their son on their own land in 2017. They didn't leave. They built a sanctuary. The llama was already named Justine.

By Matt Headley  ·  Southern Legends

The willow tree is still there.

Michele Hornsby will show it to you if you ask. She doesn't make you ask twice. The spot where they found Justin is shaded now, and the branches hang the way willow branches do — like they're listening for something they've already heard.

Justin Hornsby was twenty-six years old when he died by suicide on March 21, 2017, on the same thirty-eight acres where his parents still live, on Lenlock Lane in Anniston. His last words to Michele: I'm going to take a walk. I love you.

· · ·

Metal redbirds at the farm entrance — the bird that kept returning to the willow tree.

Before that, they'd spent more than a decade trying to bring him back from whatever had its hands on him. Drug addiction. Louisiana. First-degree burns on his neck and arms and torso that sent them driving across state lines. He came home. Detoxed. Was doing well for a time. Then he wasn't.

You know how these stories go. You've probably lived some version of one.

· · ·

After Justin died, Ray Hornsby told Michele they ought to get some goats. Clear the back land. Give her something to do with her hands. This is how some men say I love you and I don't know what else to do in the same breath. She agreed.

They got goats. Then more animals. Then they drove all the way to Oklahoma to buy a llama.

The llama was already named Justine.

Michele took that as a sign. I believe her. Some things don't need to be argued. They need to be received.

Justine arrived pregnant. She gave birth near the spot where Justin was found, on International Bereaved Mothers Day.

"Our animals have caught millions of our tears and consoled us on our darkest days."

— Ray & Michele Hornsby

They named the farm after two things: the willow tree Michele planted where they found their son, and the redbirds that kept returning to its branches after he was gone. In the rural South, a redbird after a death means something. Ask anyone who's lost someone.

· · ·

One of the farm's alpacas. There is something in being allowed near a creature that doesn't need anything from you.

Redbird Willow Farm opened in 2020. USDA licensed, 501(c)3 nonprofit. There are 120 animals on this land — alpacas, llamas, ponies, goats, rabbits, emus, Highland cattle, miniature horses, miniature donkeys, Valais blacknose sheep, peacocks, hedgehogs, a tortoise, a capybara, kangaroos. Michele keeps six rescue cats in Justin's honor. There is a joey named Kevin, bottle-fed, living in Michele's house, named after another grieving mother's son who also died.

Ray erected a thirty-foot cross at the highest point on the property. Michele runs goat yoga and llama walks and farm tours and birthday parties and grief groups and comfort visits to nursing homes and work with veterans and addiction recovery groups. Their annual Llamapalooza draws hundreds. The Calhoun County Commission honored them. CNN came down and filmed it.

None of that is why the farm exists.

The farm exists because Ray and Michele chose not to leave the land where their son died. Everything else follows from that one decision.

· · ·

Michele Hornsby. She's been at this since 2020 and she doesn't seem tired of it.

I drove out to Lenlock Lane on a July morning to talk to Michele about a bridal event I'm putting together in October. I had a pitch on my phone. I wanted to talk vendor booths and farm partnerships.

I left thinking about my own farm instead.

Heather and I grew dahlias and zinnias on a small piece of Calhoun County clay from 2021 to 2025. I lost the farm during a manic episode — the farm, and a season of my life I'm still sorting through. I know what it is to watch something you planted disappear.

What Michele and Ray lost was not a business.

And they stayed. They got the goats. They drove to Oklahoma. They accepted the llama that already had his name. Michele told me: We've always believed you should run toward the things that are most frightening, to manage them and heal in as healthy a way as you can.

That's not a philosophy. That's a thing you do with your body when you don't have any other options.

· · ·

The animals at Redbird Willow Farm will let you get close. That's what surprises people. There is something in being allowed near a creature that doesn't know your name or your year or what you lost. The goat doesn't need anything from you. The emu just looks at you.

Carrie Sexton came to the farm after her son died by suicide. She keeps coming back. She said it simply: It's amazing how an emu can help you have a much better day. That might be the most honest thing I've heard about grief in a long time.

The gift shop barn (left) and the working animal pens. They sell alpaca wool items and redbird decor.

Michele is not trying to make anyone feel better. That's not quite right. She's trying to give people a place to feel what they feel. The farm doesn't fix anything. The willow tree doesn't fix anything. The llama named Justine doesn't fix anything.

But all three are still there. And the redbirds still come.
