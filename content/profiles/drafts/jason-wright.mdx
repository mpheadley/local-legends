The day I auditioned for the Men's Chorale at Jacksonville State, Dr. Corbin had me sing down the scale. I hit a D below the bass clef staff and he turned to the bass anchor, a man I had never met, and said: "Move over."

Jason Wright moved. He laughed. No complaint, no staked-out territory, no performance of generosity. He just made room and went back to being who he had always been.

That was twenty years ago.

---

There is a kind of person who, when told to move, does it with grace. Not defeat. Not martyrdom. Just moves. Jason is that person. He was the chorale treasurer, the steady bass, the man in the first chair who made space when someone else showed up. We worked together for years at JSU and then both ended up at Anniston First United Methodist, where I was eventually his boss for four years while he directed worship. I would have called him a friend before that. What the years together in a church building made us is harder to name.

---

When I resigned in March 2025, I stopped reaching out to people. I was manic, though I would not know that for months. I thought I was fine. Better than fine. Better than ever. I resigned on Ash Wednesday and spent the next months in a kind of clarity that turned out to be psychosis. Every decision felt brilliant. Every plan felt inevitable. Then the clarity cracked.

During all of that, Jason called.

Not once. He checked on me regularly, through months when I was not responding to anyone. I was hiding. He kept calling.

He eventually took over as Worship Arts Director. The job that had been mine. He stepped into it without making it a statement about anything, without needing me to acknowledge the weight of what he was carrying, without turning his loyalty into a performance. He just did the work.

---

Last year, I built him a website.

Jason has been connected to the life of this community for a long time. Music director, worship leader, theater. He did not have a place online that told the truth about who he was. We sat down together on July 1st and talked about it. He had seen the site. He told me he felt honored and relieved.

Relieved was the word that stopped me.

Not excited. Not impressed. Relieved. Like something had been done for him that he had been waiting for without knowing how to ask.

"We often try to be clever and to impress," he said. "And it's just coming from a place of fear and insecurity."

He was talking about how most people approach their public presence, online and off. But he was also naming the opposite of what it felt like to finally have something tell the truth about who you were.

I asked what that experience was like.

"You get the privilege of being known through your design," he said.

The privilege. Not the convenience. Not the benefit. The privilege.

That is the whole thing, right there.

---

We met in the same space at the church where we have worked together for years. Jason said he was going to miss it. He had talked to Tammy, the senior pastor, about what was happening to the room. Something about the space was changing. He didn't know what was going to come next. We talked, the way you do when you have been in a building together for a long time and you can feel something shifting but you cannot name it yet.

"Letting go of the ego and letting it die," he said. He was talking about something else entirely when he said it. It landed like a line from a sermon he hadn't written yet.

---

Jason is still at Anniston First. Still making music. Still doing the work of helping people say the true thing about themselves from a stage or a chancel or wherever they find themselves trying to say it.

Twenty years ago he moved over.

He is still in the room.
