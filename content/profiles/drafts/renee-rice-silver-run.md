# The Bell Still Works

*A draft profile — Southern Legends*

---

The outhouse has three holes.

Renee Rice says this the way you'd note that a house has a good roof — as a fact that tells you something, not a curiosity to be explained. It was a schoolhouse first. Children needed somewhere to go. Three holes meant three could go at once. Boys up front, they figured. Girls in back.

The schoolhouse was built in 1850. It still has its original pews.

The bell still works.

---

Renee was not born to preserve things. She was born to build them. By her mid-forties she had a travel agency on McIntosh Road in Oxford, a husband who had been practicing law longer than most of her clients had been alive, and a piece of land in Munford with a 150-year-old schoolhouse on it that she had just converted into a wedding chapel.

Then she had a baby.

"God was like, you're shutting down, girl," said the woman who had been showing me around the chapel that afternoon. She was Renee's sister, one of the women who had watched the whole thing happen. "She had a baby and everything stopped. She couldn't do this too. It was just too much."

Cheyenne is nineteen now. At the University of Alabama. And the chapel has sat for most of her childhood the way a good coat sits in a closet — taken out for the occasional occasion, mostly just waiting.

---

Silver Run Wedding Chapel sits on nine acres in Talladega County, which is one of those sentences that doesn't tell you anything until you're standing in it. There is a building that was once a schoolhouse and is now a chapel with white pews and plank floors and windows that open onto a yard that rolls gently toward the road. There is a Roebuck House — early 1900s, two columns, wraparound porch — where Renee's eighty-seven-year-old mother lives. There is an outhouse with three holes that nobody uses anymore and an outbuilding where someone left a set of candelabras and what might be her easels. There is a barn down on McIntosh Road, a mile or two north, big enough to store most of a life.

The barn belongs to Renee too.

The barn is full of things Renee's family put there over the years — centerpieces and totem poles and silver trays that she would not be able to tell you the provenance of now. Her co-owner sister Robbie is the keeper of that inventory. Robbie knows how many of each size she has. Renee says she could not tell you any of it "no more than a man for the money."

The Rices do not own the same way. Renee builds; Robbie keeps track. Renee has the vision; Robbie has the spreadsheet. Renee called their family in to come get their furniture and turn the clean-out into a party. Robbie was in Africa the day I came to walk the property.

She sent pictures of the cheetahs.

---

Renee Rice has been in the travel business for twenty-three years. She is a Sandals affiliate. She books the kind of vacations where a multi-generational family spends $50,000 and sends her photographs of the beaches for months afterward. She has a system that segments her database by what clients have booked, their apparent wealth, and their demonstrated preferences, and when Collette Delta Vacations runs a special, the right email goes to the right person without her touching it.

She is not sentimental about any of this. She is practical, which is a different thing.

"I can make more money sitting here working on a sales booking," she told me, "than I can spending the same amount of time on a website."

This is the logic of a person who runs two businesses and has a husband who just turned seventy-one and a class reunion where she noticed that all the single people who showed up were women.

"I'm figuring out ways to get home and stay home," she said. "Come home one or two days. Work to one or two in the afternoon. Go home." She said this without wistfulness. As a plan. The way you plan to reroof a house before winter.

---

She was not trying to sell me something when she talked about the chapel. This is the thing that surprised me. Most people who are trying to divest of a property tell you how great it is. Renee told me she could not afford to open the door for $200. She told me her cousin Haley had done a birthday party down there that was over the top and probably should be renting the space on a regular basis, but nobody had time to run it. She told me that the minimum she could get per month, realistically, was $2,500 if someone wanted it exclusive. She told me she just wanted it used.

"I just want it to be used," she said.

It is a phrase she returned to more than once. Not: I want to make money. Not: I want to see it done right. I just want it used.

There is a difference, and I kept thinking about it on the drive home.

---

The pews were brought in from somewhere. Nobody is quite sure where. The original schoolhouse did not have pews; schoolhouses had benches. At some point in the decades between 1850 and when Renee's family bought the property, someone decided the building should be a chapel and brought in pews to match the story they were telling.

The bell, Renee says, they did not bring in. The bell was always there. She had it restored. It works. Nobody replaced it with a recording.

She says this the same way she says the outhouse has three holes — not to make a point, just as a fact that tells you something about the kind of person she is.

She is the kind of person who restores the original bell. She is also the kind of person who knows that the minimum to open the door is $500 before you count power and water and labor.

Both of these things are true at the same time.

---

Before I left, she said she was going to get me a Silver Run key.

"I'll get you a silver run key," she said. "I need somebody other than my guy probably. We might change those locks." She was already thinking through the logistics — Robbie's master key, Brian and the doors, the candelabras in the outbuilding, the list of things that needed to come out before the things that needed to go in.

She wanted us to go do inventory. Write down what needed to be gone, what would be used. Then throw a party for the family to come get their things.

"Make it a fun day," she said. "And send them home with all their things."

That is the Renee Rice model. You clean something out by making it a celebration. You close one chapter by throwing a party for it.

She has been doing this a long time. She knows that's how the next chapter starts.
