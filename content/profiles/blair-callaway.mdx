There is a category of music teacher that does not get enough credit. Not the one who conducts the big orchestra or leads the opera program or has their name on the concert hall. The one who shows up to a high school in a county that doesn't have much of a music budget and builds something out of the students who are actually there.

Blair Callaway spent twenty-eight years being that person.

---

## What He Did

Callaway taught instrumental music education at Jacksonville State University, which meant he was training people to go into schools and do the thing he'd already spent decades doing himself. There is a particular authority in that. He was not describing a pedagogy. He was handing you a map he'd already walked.

Before JSU, he spent those twenty-eight years in high school band programs across Alabama. Marching band, concert band, the whole architecture of public school music that most people don't think about unless they were in it — in which case they think about it constantly.

High school band is not a metaphor. It is an actual social structure with actual social consequences. The kid who gets the first chair. The freshman who can't march in step yet. The way a section sounds when it finally locks in. Callaway understood all of that, and he worked inside it with the patience that comes from having done it long enough to know that patience is the only tool that works.

---

## The Organ at Anniston UMC

He also played organ at Anniston United Methodist Church. This is the other dimension of a certain kind of Alabama musician: the Sunday morning dimension, the liturgical one, where the music is not a performance but a practice.

The pipe organ is a specific instrument. It is slow to respond and unforgiving about timing and produces a sound that goes directly into your body in a way that is difficult to explain to people who have never sat in a sanctuary while it was played well. Callaway sat at that bench and made the room do what rooms are supposed to do in church.

---

## Northeast Alabama Music at That Moment

The years when Callaway was working at JSU overlapped with a particular density of musical activity in northeast Alabama — the JSU opera program under Dr. Patricia Corbin, the show band at Gadsden State, church quartets and singing conventions and the whole informal network of people who could sing or play and were doing it everywhere.

He was inside that network. When you talk to people who were making music in Calhoun County or Etowah County or the surrounding area during that period, his name comes up.

The region has always produced more musicians than it gets credit for. Callaway was one of the people who made that happen.
