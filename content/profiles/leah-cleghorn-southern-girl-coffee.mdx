Leah Cleghorn's grandfather drank coffee all day.

He kept a pot going from morning until evening — the kind of household where coffee was not a morning ritual but a constant presence, something the house just did. She started drinking it with him as a child. They would sit together and talk. She remembers those conversations. They shaped something.

After college, she came home to Oxford with a dream that was both ambitious and specific: not just to serve coffee, but to roast her own beans. To build something from the ground up, literally. To understand what happened before the cup.

---

## The Gas Grill Years

Her stepfather Bobby Jones helped her convert an old gas grill into a backyard roaster. They learned to roast one pound at a time. This is how specialty coffee roasting is often learned — through proximity and failure, through adjusting the heat and smelling what's happening and starting again.

Eventually she upgraded to a 25-kilogram Guatemalan Probat clone manufactured in 2004 by Logos Mechanicos in Guatemala — a real commercial roaster, the kind that serious roasters use, the kind that means she stopped being someone who learned on a gas grill and became someone who roasts coffee at scale.

---

## The Shasta Trailer

She found a vintage Shasta camping trailer.

She converted it into a mobile coffee truck. She sold at downtown Oxford farmers markets, at festivals, at events across the region. The trailer became the visual identity of Southern Girl Coffee before there was a brand — before there was a name or a color scheme or a logo. It was just the trailer and the coffee and Leah inside it.

---

## Brad

At Oxfordfest in 2013, Leah handed a man named Brad Cleghorn a cup of coffee and a business card.

He was hesitant. He came back to the downtown market in subsequent weeks. They have run the business together since, and they have been married, and the coffee made all of it happen — the meeting, the business, the life.

This is not a marketing story. This is how small businesses in small Alabama cities actually work: the thing becomes the life and the life becomes the thing and at some point you can't tell them apart.

---

## The Alley Coffee Shop

Southern Girl Coffee now has a brick-and-mortar shop at 31 E. Choccolocco Street in downtown Oxford — in an alley, deliberately. The Alley Coffee Shop. The location is intentional: slow down, find the side door, make a choice to be somewhere instead of passing through.

For winter, they added heated geodome "snow globes" — outdoor domes for cold-weather seating. She has described the decision as what small businesses have to do to stay ahead: "As a small business, you kind of have to stay ahead of things. You have to think outside the box." The geodomes became one of the most photographed features of Oxford's downtown in winter.

She roasts specialty beans. She offers mobile catering for weddings and corporate events. She partners with Feazell Family Farm on "Cows and Coffee" agritourism events. She is listed by Alabama News Center as an Alabama Maker.

---

## What She Is Building

Southern Girl Coffee is a woman-owned specialty roaster in a Calhoun County city. It is the Shasta trailer grown up, the backyard gas grill grown up, the cup of coffee handed to a stranger at a festival grown up into a coffee shop in an alley that added geodomes because winter should not be a reason to stop sitting outside.

Her grandfather's pot was always going. She found a way to keep it going.
