Nick Trammell's father was Mark Trammell, baritone for Gold City — one of the quartets that defined Southern Gospel for a generation. Southern Gospel has its own canon, its own stars, its own network of churches and radio programs and bus routes across the South. Mark Trammell was inside that world in the serious way. Nick grew up inside it too.

When Nick and I met at Gadsden State, he was looking to sing. So was I.

---

## The Group

We were four: Nick Trammell, Justin Bearden, Brandon, and me. We called ourselves Narrow Way.

The name comes from the verse in Matthew — "narrow is the way that leads to life, and few there be that find it." It is a standard piece of Southern Gospel theology, the kind of phrase that appears on church signs and revival banners across the region. We were young men who had grown up singing in churches, and it felt like the right kind of name for what we were doing.

We practiced in the way you practice when you don't have a studio and you don't have much money: in living rooms and church fellowship halls and wherever someone would let us run through the parts. Southern Gospel harmony is specific. The bass goes low and the tenor goes high and the baritone fills the middle and the lead carries the melody. You have to know where you live in that structure. We did.

---

## The Circuit

We sang in churches all over northeast Alabama. Small congregations in Etowah County, Calhoun County, the places where people came out on a Sunday evening for a singing and sat in folding chairs and listened hard.

The women in those churches always had food. This is not a detail — this is the entire context. The food after a singing is part of the performance. You stand in someone's fellowship hall eating potato salad and they tell you which song they liked and you learn what actually landed.

We drove everywhere. Nick had a vehicle. I don't remember much about the driving except that there was a lot of it and that it was fine, the way things are fine when you are young and the stakes feel clear and you are doing the thing you are supposed to be doing.

---

## What I Remember

I remember that when Nick sang, it was obvious he had grown up in a house where this was serious. Not formal — serious. The difference matters. Formal is about appearance. Serious is about what happens when nobody is watching.

His father had sung on stages that we were nowhere near. But the sound was there in Nick's voice, the weight of it, the understanding of what the music was for.

I have recordings somewhere. Cassette tapes, maybe. I haven't found them yet. When I do, I will know immediately what year it was by the quality of the sound.
