The willow tree is still there. Michele Hornsby planted it where they found Justin — on March 17, 2017, on the thirty-eight acres on Lenlock Lane in Anniston where she and Ray have lived for years. He was twenty-six. She says his last words to her were: *I'm going to take a walk. I love you.*

What Ray and Michele built on that land afterward is Redbird Willow Farm — a 501(c)(3) nonprofit healing farm with 120 animals, a monthly grief support group for bereaved parents, and a quiet understanding that land that has held grief can also hold celebration. The two are not opposites. That insight is, in some ways, the whole philosophy of the farm.

The farm offers event and wedding space for couples who want something more than a venue with nice lighting and a parking lot. Redbird Willow is a working farm with animals and gardens and the specific atmosphere that comes from a place that has been through something real. Couples who choose it tend to be drawn to what it represents as much as what it looks like — the recognition that joy and grief occupy the same ground, that the land where hard things happened can become the land where beautiful things happen too. Michele spent thirty years in sales before Justin died. She will tell you she was not planning to run a farm. Ray suggested they get some goats to clear the back land, to give her something to do with her hands. One goat became more goats, and the goats became donkeys and ducks and chickens and peacocks, and the land that had held so much grief slowly filled with animals and people who needed to be near living things. The farm hosts monthly healing circles for bereaved parents — because Anniston, like every community, has more of them than anyone talks about.
