There is a version of this story that gets told at a podium, and it is not the true one. The podium version has a ribbon and a number on it, decades of service, thousands of families, a milestone worth a plaque. The true one is smaller than that, and heavier. It is one phone, ringing at 2 a.m., and a woman deciding for the ten-thousandth time to answer it.

For more than forty years, 2nd Chance, Inc. has been the place in Anniston you call when there is no one else to call. When leaving means leaving with children and a trash bag. When the safest thing in your life has become the address on your own driver's license.

<PullQuote>The doors have never been the hard part. Keeping them open has.</PullQuote>

## What Forty Years Actually Looks Like

2nd Chance, Inc. is a 501(c)(3) that serves survivors of domestic and sexual violence across Northeast Alabama, Calhoun, Cleburne, Etowah, Talladega, and Cherokee counties. It runs two emergency shelters: the Joy Kathryn Courtney House and Legacy House. It answers a crisis line around the clock. It sends advocates into courtrooms to stand next to people filing for protection orders, because the walk from the parking lot to the courthouse door is one of the loneliest walks there is, and no one should make it alone.

The list of what they do is long, and each line is a person: emergency shelter for families fleeing abuse. Court and legal advocacy. Case management. Free, trauma-informed counseling. Elder abuse support, for the survivors nobody thinks to picture. Child advocacy. Rapid rehousing paired with workforce development, so a fresh start is an actual start and not just a different place to be afraid. Community outreach and training. And, quietly, support for the pets people are terrified to leave behind, because an abuser knows a dog can be a hostage.

Everything is free. That word does a lot of work. It means the organization runs on grants that expire, donations that fluctuate, and two fundraisers a year, the Christmas gift-wrapping table at the mall, and the October gala. Alabama has only sixteen domestic violence organizations with shelters in the entire state. 2nd Chance is one of them. Subtract it, and a five-county region loses its safe room.

## The Ones Who Stayed

Institutions do not survive forty years. People do, one at a time, showing up on the days it would have been easier not to.

Executive Director Krychelle Smith runs the current operation, and she is plain about how the work gets done: "Volunteers can help with support groups or answer calls on our crisis line." It is not a glamorous ask. It is the ask that keeps a shelter alive, a human being, awake, willing to pick up.

Behind the current staff is a longer memory. Every organization that lasts this long has people who were there before the name was even settled, who can still tell you which room the first family slept in and what it cost that year to keep the lights on. That memory is not on the website. It walks around in the women who never left, the ones who answered the phone in the early years and are still answering it now. Their names rarely make the newsletter. They are the reason there is a newsletter.

## In Her Words

The survivors are the ones the whole thing is for, and their names stay off the page on purpose, safety is not a formality here. One of them put forty years into two sentences:

<PullQuote>I showed up with my kids and two trash bags. They gave us a room, and then they gave us a life.</PullQuote>

That is the entire mission, compressed. A room is the emergency. A life is the work. The distance between them is measured in court dates, counseling sessions, a rehousing check, a job, a first month where the phone does not make you flinch. 2nd Chance has walked that distance with people for four decades, and the reason it can is that somebody, every single night, decided to answer.

## Why This Room Matters

I built the gala page for 2nd Chance as a gift, a place where neighbors can take a seat at the table that funds all of this. But the page is not the point, and neither is the gala. The point is the math nobody puts on a flyer: a five-county region, sixteen shelters in the whole state, and one local organization holding the door in Anniston. Every dollar that comes through the October room stays here, funding beds, advocates, and counseling for people who live down the road from you.

Forty years is not a bureaucratic milestone. It is a promise that has been kept ten thousand nights in a row, mostly in the dark, mostly by people whose names you will never see.
