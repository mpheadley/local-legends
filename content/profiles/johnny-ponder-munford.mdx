There is a sound Calhoun County makes on a Friday night in the fall, and if you grew up here you can hear it with your eyes closed. The band. The chains. The far-off roar when the home team breaks one loose. And under all of it, riding an AM signal and a little FM translator up out of Anniston, a voice saying the names out loud — Jacksonville, Lincoln, White Plains — like they matter. Because to the man saying them, they do.

That voice belongs to Johnny Ponder.

I met him at a folding tent at the Woodstock 5K, and I'll be honest about what I carried up to it: a bias I hadn't earned. I run a little local-storytelling thing myself. I've watched what happens to *local* — watched it get bought and automated and shipped in from a server in another state — and somewhere along the way I'd decided that a man standing under a "Classic Station" banner on a Saturday morning was a relic. A holdout. Somebody keeping a dead thing warm.

Then Mr. Ponder offered me a biscuit.

He had a sack of Jack's biscuits in his truck and was handing them out like it was the whole reason he'd come, and inside of ten minutes he'd undone every assumption I walked up with. He wanted to hear about my podcast — said we ought to collab. He told me a joke about Baptists and Methodists I'm not going to print here. He turned an offhand line about my ukulele into a real conversation about baritone tuning. And when I mentioned Wendell Berry, this radio man — this *relic* — turned out to be a forty-year forester who could tell you exactly how a Bradford pear thorn goes through a work boot. One had gone through his.

"I had an unfair bias, I suppose," I said out loud, half to the live stream and half to myself. *He's with it. He's with the times. He's trending.* The man I'd written off before I met him was the most alive person at the race.

<PullQuote>A local voice, live, on a Friday night, in a county most of America can't find and a medium most of America stopped listening to. That's the whole story.</PullQuote>

## The Last Local Voice

You have to understand what has happened to radio to understand what Johnny Ponder is.

Most of the dial got bought. It got consolidated, automated, and piped in from a studio in another state — the same forty songs, the same syndicated host pretending to be local, the same voice reading the same liner in a hundred markets at once. The local station, the one that knew your ballteam and your weather and read the lunch menu, mostly died. Somebody decided it wasn't worth the money.

Johnny Ponder is what's left when somebody decided it *was.*

He's on **95.1 The Mountain** — WDNG, licensed to Anniston, classic hits since the spring of 2021, carried on 95.1 FM and old 1450 AM, owned by Lake Broadcasting. "East Central Alabama's Classic Station," the tent says. The home for everything NASCAR. Alabama Crimson Tide football and basketball. And then the part that tells you what kind of station it really is: **Jacksonville High football and basketball. Lincoln High football. White Plains basketball.**

White Plains basketball. Nobody syndicates White Plains basketball. There is no server in Texas that knows those kids' names. Johnny does. He and his colleague were out at the race that morning for the same reason they show up to the ballgames — "just to have a presence," he said, "to be part of it." ## Before the Mic, the Forest

The voice is the second thing Johnny Ponder built that outlasted the people who said it couldn't be done. The first was a forest, and a way of teaching kids to love it.

He grew up in Munford at the foot of Cheaha Mountain — boys on bikes, fishing creeks, arrowheads in plowed fields, bows cut from creek-side saplings. He writes about it now, short and plain, for Alabama Pioneers: "Growing up in Munford, Alabama in the 1950s and 1960s." A boyhood that lasted maybe twenty years in Alabama's particular geography before it was gone.

Then he went into the woods for a living. **Forty years a forester.**

It hasn't left him. Standing at that tent, thirty-some years off the job, he could still name the enemies without pausing — kudzu and Chinese privet, the two that eat Alabama alive; the Bradford pear, thorny as hell, grafted to something invasive underneath, its seeds carried and dropped by birds because nothing native will eat its leaves. He'd fought all of it. He told me about the pear thorn that punched clean through his boot and into his foot, the way another man might mention an old ballgame. When I nodded at a strange tree across the lot he squinted at it and offered a guess — a monkey puzzle, maybe — the reflex of a man who has spent his life reading a landscape.

And then he came home.

## What He Built When He Got Back

Johnny Ponder joined the **Talladega County School Board in November 1994**, District 3 — the district that includes Munford, where he went to school himself. He's been there ever since.

In that time he was central to building something almost no other public school system in Alabama has: a **130-acre outdoor education campus** at the base of Cheaha, right against the Talladega National Forest. Twelve outdoor classrooms. An amphitheater. A nature trail, a longleaf pine stand, a low ropes course, a greenhouse, a fish pond, a frog pond, a five-tier wetland, butterfly gardens, a bluebird trail, an aquaculture facility. Inside the buildings: a lobby built to look like a cave, hallways with life-sized trees, a cafeteria under murals of the national forest, a 250-gallon aquarium.

The Alabama Farmers Federation held Munford up as a model. Georgia-Pacific and the Forest Workforce Training Institute sent materials. It drew statewide notice for the least glamorous reason there is: it worked. Kids were learning biology and ecology by walking into a forest older than the county.

His own words for the whole ambition: *"We're just trying to encourage kids to get off their laptops and phones and do some stuff outside."*

<PullQuote>The through line is not hard to find. It's the forest — and then it's the voice. Both are the same instinct: keep the local thing alive, and hand it to whoever comes next.</PullQuote>

## The Long Logic

Look at it whole and the pattern is plain. He grew up in the forest at the foot of Cheaha. He worked in it forty years. He came home and built a school around it so Munford's children could learn what he learned, in the same landscape where he learned it.

And on Friday nights and race weekends, he does the same thing with a microphone that he did with the woods — he keeps a local thing alive and says it out loud, so the next person will know it was here. The grandmother who can't drive to the away game hears her grandson's name because Johnny said it. That's not nostalgia. That's a man refusing to let the local disappear quietly just because the market says it should.

Here's the part I got wrong. I'd expected a man mourning his medium, worried it ends with him. What I found was the opposite — somebody entirely uninterested in being a monument. He wanted to talk about starting a podcast. He wanted to collab. "You're in the storytelling business," he told me, "same as us," and he meant it as an invitation, not an epitaph. The threat to local radio isn't that its keepers are too sentimental to change. It's that there aren't enough of them left who care enough to bother. Johnny cares enough to bother. ## Munford, Still

Johnny Ponder is a member at Anniston First United Methodist. His daughter is Emmalie Ponder Whitney. He's a teetotaler — which, if you've ever stood in the infield at Talladega on a race weekend, is its own quiet kind of legend. He is, as more than one person in this county will tell you without prompting, the one and only.

Before I left he pressed another biscuit on me and told me I ought to come by the station sometime, sit in on the show. I said I'd like that. We talked a minute more — about aging, mostly; I told him about turning forty and blowing out a knee and the hard year that came with it, and he waved it off. "My worst decade was my sixties," he said, and grinned like a man who'd already decided the next one would be better. Then he went back to manning the tent, biscuits and all, a forty-year forester under a Classic Station banner, still saying the names out loud for anyone who cared to listen. I'd come to write off a relic. I drove home having met the most local man in the county — and I was the one who left corrected.
