There was a boy named Willie Tichenor who died of bone cancer at nineteen.

Before he died, somebody asked him what he wanted done with the money that would have been his — the trust, the future, whatever a nineteen-year-old is owed by the life he isn't going to get to live. He asked for two things. He asked his family to fund cancer research, so that other people might have the options he didn't. And he asked them to send young people on mission trips, because serving other people had changed his own short life more than anything else had.

His family took the question he'd been asked — *what would Willie want?* — and made it the name of a foundation. Quad W. What Would Willie Want. It funds cancer research around the world, and it funds an eight-week summer internship that puts college students into poor neighborhoods to live in community and serve.

For about a year, I was the person deciding whether to bring that internship to Anniston.

## Ministry With, Not Ministry For

The QuadW Missional Internship was the first American expression of Forge, the missional-church movement that Alan Hirsch and Mike Frost started in Australia. If you've spent time around church-renewal language, you know the word *missional* has been worn smooth by overuse. Forge meant something specific by it. Not the church running programs *for* the poor from a safe distance — the church living *with*, in the neighborhood, at the same table, on the same schedule.

The interns don't parachute in for a week. They come for eight. They live together, usually in a house in the neighborhood they're serving, and they hold to a rhythm that sounds almost monastic when you lay it out — lights out at eleven, up early, phones off during the workday, Scripture and journaling and accountability groups, real conflict with the people they're crammed into a house with. The founders had a word for the discomfort of it: *liminality.* The in-between place. You have to leave the room you're comfortable in before you'll grow in any direction that matters.

The program launched in Mobile in 2009 and spread — Birmingham, Huntsville, Dallas, Kansas City, Pine Bluff, Elkhart, Tampa, Reading, a site across the border in Nuevo Laredo. The stipend was the same everywhere: twenty-five hundred dollars for the summer. The idea was the same everywhere too. Put young people in the liminal place, hand them real work among people the church usually drives past, and let the summer do to them what a summer like that does.

## Glen Addie

The Anniston site would have run out of two worksites, and the first one was New Life United Methodist Church, in Glen Addie.

Glen Addie is a public housing community. New Life is a rebirth of the old Glen Addie Community Church, which started back in the mid-sixties when a group of volunteers from First United Methodist downtown came over to help elementary and junior-high kids in the projects with their schoolwork. That's the whole origin — some Methodists from the nice church downtown deciding the kids across town deserved somebody to sit with them over their homework. Sixty years later the needs in that neighborhood are the same ones they always were: food, literacy, childcare, somebody in the corner of a child who is used to being nobody's priority.

By the time I was looking at it, New Life was feeding more than a hundred people lunch every Saturday at noon. Summer kids' camp. A summer feeding program. Community meals. Monthly groceries. It was, as one person in my notes put it bluntly, the only church in Anniston actually doing ministry *with* the community instead of *for* it. The conference didn't always understand what was happening on the ground there. The people doing it had to fight to keep it afloat — chase the grant deadlines, make the case again every year for why you'd invest in a ministry that would never be "sustainable" in the way a spreadsheet wants.

The second worksite was the Sable Learning Center, run by Community Enabler, and it sat in a place worth knowing about: Hobson City.

Hobson City is a small municipality just south of Anniston — a few hundred people. It was the first self-governed, all-Black town in Alabama. When the surrounding white district redrew its lines in 1899 to push Black residents out, those residents did the thing nobody expected: they incorporated their own town and elected their own government. Sable Learning Center is a free program for children there — reading, life skills, anger management, computer training — most of them from low-income homes, fed breakfast and lunch and snacks through the summer. You do not get to work in Hobson City without brushing up against the whole century of history that made it necessary.

That was the ground QuadW would have put its interns on. Not a mission trip to somewhere photogenic. Glen Addie and Hobson City, all summer.

## The Year I Spent Deciding

I didn't come to it on my own. [Rev. Dr. Adlene Kufarimai](/profiles/adlene-kufarimai) brought it to me.

She held the conference portfolio for Ministry with the Poor, and part of what she did — part of what she's always done — was find younger clergy and hand them things bigger than they'd have reached for on their own. In the spring of 2019 she started asking me to pray about becoming the QuadW site coordinator for Anniston in 2020. I wrote to her in May of that year telling her I was still praying about it, could she send me the dates and a rough sketch of the job. She wrote back the whole list — recruit the interns, build a team of mentors, secure housing, line up churches to cook the meals, keep the finances clean, file the reports, meet with Don when he came for site visits — and closed it the way she closes things. *Whatever you hear God saying in this time of discernment, it is well with me.*

The site director training was in Dallas that September. Two days, the eleventh and the twelfth. Don Woolley ran the national program, and Don Worley had already come to Anniston twice to look at New Life and pronounced it ideal — exactly the kind of place QuadW was built for. The interns themselves would have started the summer up the mountain at [Camp Sumatanga](/profiles/camp-sumatanga), the conference camp outside Gadsden, for a week of orientation before coming down to the neighborhood — the deep breath before the plunge.

I was pastoring Weaver First United Methodist Church at the time. Our line was *a garden of discipleship, empowering our community to flourish in body, mind, and spirit* — which is a nice thing to put on a sign and a hard thing to actually mean. QuadW was one of the ways I was trying to figure out whether I meant it. Whether the church I was serving could be the kind that goes to Glen Addie instead of waiting for Glen Addie to come to it.

By April of 2020 I was still on it — working down a contact list of interns, following up on a letter from Don.

You know what April of 2020 was.

## What the Pandemic Ended, and What It Didn't

The summer that needed a hundred people packed into a lunchroom and a house full of interns sharing bathrooms was the exact summer the world was told to stay six feet apart and not share anything with anyone. The discernment didn't get a clean ending. It got the ending everything got that year.

I've gone back and forth on whether that was a loss. Some of it plainly was — the interns who didn't get their summer, the kids in Glen Addie who didn't get theirs. But the thing QuadW was trying to teach me didn't require the internship to run. It required me to keep it, and I did.

*Ministry with, not ministry for.* The refusal to serve people from a safe distance. The conviction that the transformation runs both directions — that the college kid from the suburbs and the child in the projects are both being changed by the same eight weeks, and neither one is the project. Liminality — that you have to leave the comfortable room. The idea that a nineteen-year-old dying of cancer could look at what little was left to him and decide the best use of it was to make sure other people got *disrupted* toward each other.

I build things now that are not churches, mostly. But the DNA that Adlene handed me in a parking-lot conversation and Willie's family handed the whole program from a deathbed — [the limp](/essays/trust-the-one-who-walks-with-a-limp), the walking-alongside, the flat refusal to stand above the people you claim to serve — that's still the whole thing. I never got to run the Anniston site. I've been running its argument ever since.
