# Made Here

Wendell Berry keeps a distinction I can't put down. He borrowed the words from Wallace Stegner, but he made them a way of seeing: there are boomers and there are stickers. The boomer comes to a place to take what he can and move on. The sticker stays. He learns the ground, marries into the trouble of it, and spends his life tending what he could never carry off. One measures a place by what it will yield him. The other measures himself by what he can give back to it.

I have spent a lot of my life not sure which one I was. I left. I came back. And somewhere in the coming back I started paying attention to how little of what we buy is made anywhere near where we live.

Think about a t-shirt. Your church prints one for the fall festival, or your kid's team needs jerseys, or the little shop downtown finally wants its logo on something. Where does it come from? A warehouse in another state, most likely. The design gets uploaded to a company that could be anywhere, printed by a machine tended by no one you'll ever meet, and shipped back to a town it never actually touched. The shirt says your town's name on it and was made as far from your town as the system could manage. The money followed the shirt out the door and did not come back.

This is what Berry called the total economy. It treats every place as the same place, every worker as interchangeable, every thing as a thing that could have been made anywhere and therefore belongs nowhere. It is very good at being cheap. It is very bad at keeping a community whole, because a community is exactly the thing it cannot see.

Here is the part I keep turning over. We are told the newest tools, automation, print-on-demand, and now the AI I use every day, are going to finish the job. Make everything even more placeless. Strip even more of the work out of the towns and into the cloud. And they can. That is one direction to point them.

But a tool doesn't choose its direction. We do.

So I built a thing, and I'm still building it, called Gather Made. The plain version: when your shop or your church or your family needs something real, a shirt, a book, a keepsake, it routes the job to a maker and a printer in the same county first. A local designer draws it. A local shop with a heat press prints it. The money stays close enough to see. And it gets stamped with two words that have to be true: made here.

I want to be honest about the limp in it, because Berry would want me to be. It is not pure. If you're buying from three states away, sometimes the sensible thing is a national printer, and I use one as a fallback. But a shirt made in a warehouse never gets to wear the words "made here." That would be a lie, and the honesty is the whole point. I also run the thing with AI, which some people will find funny coming from a man quoting an agrarian who writes by hand. I don't think the machine is the enemy. I think placelessness is the enemy, and a machine can be aimed at a place as easily as away from it. The question is never whether the tool is new. The question is whether it serves the ground you're standing on or strips it.

Berry has another word I lean on: membership. In his stories, people don't have accounts, they have a membership in a place and in each other. That's the thing I actually want to build. Not a marketplace where strangers extract value from strangers, but a small, visible web where the maker knows the printer knows the shop knows the buyer, and every transaction leaves the town a little more woven together instead of a little more hollowed out.

People keep asking me to pick a side. Am I the man who quotes an agrarian poet by lamplight, or the one building software marketplaces with AI after midnight? Both. I can speak fluent tech-bro and fluent Wendell Berry, and I've quit apologizing for the seam between them. Agrarianism and cyberpunk don't cancel out in my head. They make a chord. For the last year and a half I've watched myself do the same thing over and over: take two poles everyone treats as enemies and hold them until they reconcile. That is the gathering instinct, and it is exactly why the company is named what it's named. I want the things that got split apart put back together.

Berry writes a good deal about cooperatives, neighbors pooling what they have so no one family carries the whole risk. It already happens here. A man named Sam Sawyer ties a local meat market to a web of local growers, and did the same for a flower farm I love. That is the real shape under all of this. Not a platform that owns the makers and rents them back their own audience, but something closer to a co-op that belongs to the people in it. The infrastructure can be mine to tend. The membership belongs to the town.

He said, in the end, that it all turns on affection. You take care of what you love, and you can only love what you actually know, which means what is near enough to know. That is not a business plan. But it turned out to be one anyway.

I don't know if I'm a sticker yet. I think you earn that word slowly, by staying when leaving would be easier, by tending a place long past the point it pays you back. But I know which direction I want to point the tools I have. Toward home. Made here.
