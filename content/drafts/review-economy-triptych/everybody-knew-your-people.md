# Everybody Knew Your People

Before there were stars, there was your grandmother.

If you wanted to know whether the man who said he could fix your roof could actually fix your roof, you didn't read anything. You asked. And the answer came back wrapped in three generations of information: *He's Ferrell's boy, they've been doing roofs out past Bynum forever, but call the daddy not the son.* That was the review. It was slow, it was partial, it was soaked in grudges and loyalties that had nothing to do with roofing, and it was almost impossible to fake, because to fake it you'd have had to fake being from here, and everybody knew your people.

I left, and then I came back, and coming back is what let me see it.

When you grow up inside a thing you can't feel its shape. You just breathe it. It took leaving Alabama, [MATT: name the diaspora arc in one line: Mission Viejo, Gabon, Egypt, North Carolina, Muncie, Lombard, whatever compression lands], and living in places where nobody knew my people and I knew nobody's, to understand what a reputation had been back home. Out there, I was a stranger with a credit score and a Yelp account. That was my whole standing in the world. A number some company assigned me and a pile of stars from people I'd never meet, about businesses run by people they'd never meet either. Everyone a stranger, vouching for strangers, to strangers.

It felt modern. It felt like freedom, at first, nobody knew my people, so nobody could hold my people against me. And then one winter it just felt like being no one. Here is what nobody tells you about the star economy: it was sold to us as an upgrade on grandma. Scalable. Objective. A million reviews instead of one biased old woman's opinion. And for a while it worked well enough that we let the old way die, stopped asking over the fence, started asking our phones.

Then the machines learned to write the stars.

Now the pile of reviews on the roofer, the restaurant, the used truck, is as likely as not to be manufactured, generated by a model, bought in bulk, warm and human-sounding and completely empty. The upgrade on grandma turned out to be a downgrade on nothing: not even a biased old woman, just a voice with no body behind it, praising a business no one has walked into. We traded a slow truth soaked in grudges for a fast lie soaked in nothing, and we called it progress because it was convenient.

I run a farmers market now. There are no stars at the market. There's a woman who'll tell you, quietly, which stand not to trust. She's biased. She holds grudges. She's almost never wrong.

Everybody knows her people.
