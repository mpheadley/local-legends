# Testimony and the False Witness

There's a commandment about reviews. We just don't call it that.

*You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor.* We file it under courtroom perjury, something that happens to other people in serious rooms. But the Hebrew is plainer and closer to the ground than that. It's about what you say about someone when they're not there to answer. It's about reputation, the thing a person can't hold in their own hands, that lives entirely in the mouths of everyone else.

Which is to say it's about reviews. It always was.

---

The church has always run on testimony. Not the star-rating kind, the standing-up-in-front-of-people kind. "Here is what happened to me. Here is what I was and what I am now." It's the oldest form of evidence we have, older than the written gospels: a person, present, staking their own name on a claim about their own life. Testimony is dangerous precisely because it can't be outsourced. You have to be there. You have to be the one it happened to.

The false witness is the opposite motion. It's a claim about a life, made by someone who wasn't in it, aimed at a person who can't respond. And here is what should stop us cold: we have built an entire economy on the false witness, and we've made it frictionless, and most of us participate in it before breakfast. Every fake five stars. Every hired one-star. Every warm, human-sounding paragraph of praise that no human being ever felt. A machine can now bear false witness at the speed of light, in the voice of your most trusted friend, and it never has to stand up in front of anyone.

[MATT: the disclosed-AI turn, but pastoral. You build software that could generate false testimony, and you disclose the machine every time, on purpose. Frame it as a spiritual discipline, not a marketing tactic. Confession as a business practice. Why telling on your own machine is a form of not bearing false witness. Keep it reported, not preached.]

---

I think the reason the review economy feels so sick, the reason a five-star rating now lands in the gut like nothing at all, is that we know, without being told, that witness has been separated from body. The whole point of testimony was that a person paid for it with their presence. They were *there.* When you sever the claim from the body that made it, you don't get more truth cheaper. You get the form of truth with the life drained out. A stained-glass window with no light behind it.

The church's stubborn, unfashionable insistence has always been the opposite: that you have to show up. That the sacraments require bodies in a room, bread you can chew, water on actual skin. That grace is not a rating you can leave from your phone. We baptize people in front of witnesses for the same reason a testimony means something, because presence is the price, and the price is the proof.

[MATT: the Ecclesia beat. You're building a fresh expression, people actually gathered, hybrid but real. Name the thing you're insisting on against the frictionless world: showing up. Bodies. The Tuesday morning. Report what that costs and what it's worth. End close to the ground.]

---

The false witness was always a way of getting the benefit of a relationship without the cost of one. The machine just made it free. And the answer the church has, the only answer it's ever had, is not a better algorithm. It's a body in a room, standing up, saying: *here is what happened to me. You can see my face. Ask me anything.*

That's testimony. Everything else is five stars from a machine that has never held the thing.

Come stand in the room.
