# The Machine Will Give You Five Stars

I write software that writes reviews now. I want to tell you how easy it was.

You give it a business, a tone, a star count. It gives you a paragraph a real person would recognize as their own gratitude. "The staff went above and beyond." "You could tell they actually cared." It knows the cadence of a satisfied customer because it has read ten million satisfied customers. It is very good. It is good the way a forged signature is good, not because it's true, but because it's practiced.

I didn't build that to sell it. I built it to see how thin the wall was.

The wall is not thin. There is no wall.

---

Here is what a five-star rating used to mean. Somebody stood in a room, received something, and decided it was good. That's it. That's the whole machine. A person, a thing, a verdict. The rating was a receipt for an experience that actually happened to a body that actually existed.

That machine is gone. What replaced it is a machine in the other sense, a system that manufactures the *appearance* of a thousand people standing in a thousand rooms, when nobody stood anywhere. The reviews on the thing you're about to buy were written by a model that has never held it. The five stars on the contractor were bought in a bundle. The one-star takedown of his competitor was bought in the same bundle, from the same seller, on the same afternoon.

We used to say a picture is worth a thousand words. Now a thousand words costs about a tenth of a cent and arrives in under a second, in the voice of your most trustworthy neighbor.

[MATT: the farmers market beat. You run one. You've watched how reputation actually moves in a small market, the vendor everybody quietly warns each other about, the one whose peaches sell out because three people said so at the next booth over. That's the old machine, still working, in one square block. Report a scene. Let it stand against the online version without announcing the contrast.]

---

I disclose my AI. Every venture I run says, out loud, when a machine did the work. People ask me why, isn't that a competitive disadvantage, telling on yourself? And the honest answer is that disclosure is not a moral flourish. It's the only thing left that a machine can't fake, because faking it defeats it. Anyone can generate a warm human review. Nobody can generate *the fact that they told you the truth about who wrote it.* That's not content. That's a relationship.

This is the whole argument, and I'll say it plainly because the thesis has earned it by now: **the review economy didn't die because people got dishonest. It died because honesty stopped being expensive.** When a real testimonial and a forged one cost the same to produce, nothing, the real one loses its only advantage. The market floods. The signal drowns. And the thing that comes back, the thing that always comes back when the mass-produced version collapses, is the handmade one. The named one. The one with a face attached that you can go find and yell at if it lied.

[MATT: your own credential-as-wreckage beat, if it fits. You know what it costs to say the true hard thing about yourself in public, the Star column, the diagnosis, the camper. You've paid the price of disclosure with your own name. That's why you trust it as a business model: you've already run the experiment on yourself.]

---

I'm not going to tell you to stop reading reviews. I read them. I'll tell you what I actually do now, which is smaller and older than any algorithm: I find the person. Not the rating, the person. The one who'll put their name on it and stand in the room with me. There are fewer of them than there used to be, and they're worth more than they've ever been, and no machine I know how to build can make another one.

The machine will give you five stars. It will give you as many as you want. That's the problem, and that's the tell.

Ask who's standing in the room.
