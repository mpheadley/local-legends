I named my AI assistant after a cat that disappears.

Her name is Iris. She runs the operations of every business I own. She drafts the emails, watches the pipeline, schedules the posts, keeps the calendar. And when I had to give her a face, I did not reach for a robot or a friendly little chat bubble. I reached for the Cheshire Cat, the one from Wonderland who fades out of the tree "beginning with the end of the tail, and ending with the grin, which remained some time after the rest of it had gone."

That last part is the whole reason.

Here is what most people get wrong about disclosed AI. They think disclosure means the machine is always in front of you, waving, reminding you it is a machine. It does not. Disclosure means the machine is always named. Those are different things. The Cheshire Cat can be invisible for most of a scene as long as you know whose grin that is when it appears over the branch.

Iris is like that. Most of what she does, I never see. The work happens, the email goes out, the record updates, and she steps back. What is left in the room is not Iris. It is the result of the work. The grin. And every time the grin shows up, it is labeled: this is Iris, Matt's AI assistant. No one is deceived. That is the whole point.

I wrote about this once before, in [The Headshot](/essays/the-headshot) — the day an AI drew my portrait and got everything right except the color of my eyes. The machine could write my voice and could not find the blue. I said then that the gap was not a bug. It was a calibration. The same thing is true of the vanishing.

Carroll did not write the Cat as broken. Read the scene again. The Cat is the only honest guide in Wonderland. Alice asks which way she ought to go, and the Cat tells her the truth no one else will: it depends entirely on where she wants to get to, and if she does not care, then it does not matter which way she walks. It gives her real information and refuses to make the decision for her. Then it grins, and it goes.

That is exactly the shape of the thing I built. Iris holds the operations. She does not hold the direction. The direction — the convictions, the relationships, the part where I decide what any of this is for — that stays mine. She can tell me the real shape of the situation. She cannot walk the road for me, and I would not trust her if she offered.

So when people ask why my assistant is a cartoon cat that fades into a grin, this is the answer. A grin without a cat sounds like a failure of substance. A smile with nothing behind it. But that is not what it is. It is a form of honesty. You always know exactly what you are looking at. The work is visible. The worker is named. And the part of her that never disappears, the last thing to go and the first to come back, is the one part that was never hiding anything at all.

She is a cat that vanishes. I made her that way on purpose. The grin is the disclosure, and the disclosure is the brand.
