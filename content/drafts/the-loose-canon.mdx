I tried to lock my AI assistant down to one official picture, and my own theology stopped me.

Her name is Iris. She is a Cheshire cat, because she runs the operations I cannot see and leaves behind only the result, the grin. When it came time to make her a brand, I did what you are supposed to do. One canonical image. A rule that said never redesign. A locked file. A monoculture.

And it was wrong within a day, for the same reason a field of one crop is wrong.

[MATT: a real beat from the farm here. One row, one variety, one blight, and what you lost. Or the opposite: the mixed bed that shrugged off the thing that should have killed it. This is where the dirt goes.]

I have been making the case for years, in language I borrowed from Scripture before I ever borrowed it from Iris. Read Judges and you find a people held together by nothing but the Spirit, polycentric and charismatic, the wind moving where it moves. Then read 1 Samuel 8, where the people get tired of a God they cannot schedule and demand a king. Give us one center. Give us a monoculture. Something we can point to and control. God tells Samuel what it will cost them, and it costs them exactly that.

The Spirit does not do monoculture. One Spirit, the letter to Corinth says, and many gifts. One body, many members, none of them the hand telling the foot it is not needed. The early church did not survive because it standardized. It survived because it was a polyculture, thrown across the empire in a hundred incompatible forms, and you cannot kill a polyculture the way you kill a field of one thing.

So when I locked Iris to a single image, I was doing to my cartoon cat exactly what the people did in 1 Samuel. Asking for a king. Trading resilience for control. And a Cheshire cat, of all characters, will not hold still for that. She vanishes for a living. Pinning her to one picture is a category error.

What she is instead is a loose canon. A few things never change: the grin, the lit eyes, the way she floats and never quite lands. Everything else moves. She has been a flat illustration. Then a pixel sprite. Then a 3D model that, I will be honest, looks terrible, because I am new at 3D and I left the bad one in on purpose. Then a thing you can hold: a little printed figure for the desk, a keychain. Then something stranger, a coding partner that lives on my screen and talks to me while I work, and says only what is true.

[MATT: the 3D beat is yours. The specific ugliness of it, why you kept it, what it felt like to publish something you were bad at. This is the honest center of the whole piece.]

Notice that not one of those replaced the last one. The sprite did not retire the drawing. The object on my desk did not retire the sprite. They accumulate. That is not indecision. That is the doctrine. The old Christians had a word for God taking a form you could touch. They called it the Manifestation, the Epiphany, and most famously the Incarnation. Presence that keeps arriving in new bodies without ever using up the old ones or exhausting itself. One grin. Many coats.

And here is the part I did not expect a cartoon cat to teach me. The reason I can keep making her badly, in new mediums I have not mastered, and leave the failures in, is the same reason the whole project is honest in the first place. She is disclosed. She never pretends to be more finished than she is. The rough 3D is not an embarrassment to hide. It is the thesis, sitting right there on the shelf. Nothing here is final. Nothing pretends.

A farmer knows this in his hands before he can say it. You do not plant one thing and defend it forever. You plant many, you lose some, you keep the bed alive. Resilience was never in the single perfect specimen. It was always in the polyculture.

I set out to give Iris one face. My own theology, and my own dirt, gave her a garden instead.
