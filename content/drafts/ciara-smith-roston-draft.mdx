## The Room She Grew Up In

Before she could read, her grandmother brought her to 17th Street Baptist Church to hear grown people talk about how a city changes.

Gertrude Nettles raised her. The meetings were SCLC. Ciara was five. She sat in pews that had held Rosa Parks and Thurgood Marshall, that had held Martin Luther King Jr. when he came through, and his father before him. The pastor through her childhood, Rev. Nimrod Q. Reynolds, had been beaten by a mob in 1963 on the same afternoon a bomb went off at the 16th Street Baptist Church in Birmingham. He came back the next day. The church kept the meeting going.

In 1964 the ministers at 17th Street took out a one-page ad in the local paper that read, *We want our freedom and we want it now*. They called it the Anniston Manifesto.

That is the room she grew up in. Not the metaphorical room. The actual one. A child small enough to need a booster, listening to the people who had already decided that the city she would one day run was going to have to answer for itself.

## City Hall at Sixteen

Her first job was a clerk's desk in the Anniston Finance Department. She was sixteen.

I keep coming back to that. You can spend a long time in a city and never see the inside of City Hall except for the lobby. She had a desk there before she had a driver's license. She filed budget paper while other people her age were stocking shelves at the Walmart on Highway 21. She watched a city run itself from a chair the public wasn't allowed to sit in.

Her mother, Christina Wolff, said it at the victory party years later, after the votes had been counted and the dancing had started.

<PullQuote>
She's always said she was going to be the mayor of Anniston and the president of the United States.
</PullQuote>

A mother says a lot of things. Some of them turn out to be true the way prophecies are true, by accident or repetition. This one she said often enough that her daughter believed it before anyone asked her to.

After Anniston High School in 2017 she went to Spelman. Her freshman year she went door-to-door for Keisha Lance Bottoms in Atlanta. The next year Forbes named her to its 30 Under 30 list and flew her to Boston. At nineteen she was interning for Congressman Hank Johnson in Washington. The year after that he nominated her for the Congressional Black Caucus Institute training program.

She graduated from Spelman in 2020. She came back to Anniston.

## The Ward 3 Race

The seat she came back for was held by Ben Little.

Little had been on the council a long time. In 2012 a Calhoun County judge convicted him of giving direct orders to a city employee — he had told someone to take a surveillance DVD out of a supervisor's desk — and removed him from office two and a half days before his term was set to expire. He came back. In 2018 he was indicted on ethics charges for voting on a nuisance resolution that involved properties he owned. He came back from that too.

In August of 2020 he ran for re-election in Ward 3 and Ciara Smith forced him into a runoff. She was twenty-one. On October 6 she beat him 67 percent to 33. She was sworn in on November 2, the youngest person ever elected to office in the city of Anniston. The council named her vice mayor that same week.

She would beat Ben Little again, five years later, in a five-way race for mayor. He finished third. He got 12 percent.

Twice he tried to hold ground. Twice she took it.

## The Room Where Jay Resigned

On October 15, 2024, Ward 1 councilman Jay Jenkins waited until the end of a council meeting the *Anniston Star* called "somewhat routine," and then he resigned. He had been on the council for fifteen years. He gave no notice. The other members were stunned.

The photograph the *Star* ran the next morning showed councilwoman Millie Harris hugging him. Behind them, in the background of the frame, was Ciara Smith. The caption noted her by name. Vice Mayor. In the room.

Lewis Downing was appointed to fill the seat.

She has been in every room where the next thing happens. Her predecessor's resignation, her successor council member's appointment, the morning the *Star* picked which photograph to run. She doesn't appear in the foreground of any of those frames. She was studying them.

## What She Walked Into

Jack Draper resigned the mayor's office on May 1, 2025. He had served eight and a half years. He said he needed to focus on his family, his health, and his career.

On May 6 the council appointed Ciara Smith mayor by unanimous vote. Gloria Floyd, Calhoun County's first African American female attorney, administered the oath. Floyd had taught her. She called the moment "profoundly moving."

At the same meeting, during public comment, a man named Joseph Giri stood up and addressed the new mayor about Monsanto's PCB legacy on the city's Westside, the $700 million settlement, the eleven billion in cancer claims still moving through the courts, the herbicide the city was still spraying on its lawns. He didn't soften it. He listed the chemistry and the case law.

Smith had been sworn in less than an hour. She thanked him. She said environmental responsibility would be a mandate of the office.

The room the city raised her in was answering to itself again. She was on the other side of the dais now.

## The August Victory

She ran for the seat in her own right that summer. Five candidates, including the city manager and Ben Little. She avoided a runoff. She got 57.31 percent on the first ballot.

The victory party was at the Anniston City Meeting Center. She waited until after eight to declare. Her supporters were dancing.

In the JSU alumni profile that came out a month later, she said this about the ceiling:

<PullQuote>
I was the one who shattered the glass ceiling, but there were some people who beat on it for me to get here.
</PullQuote>

There were, in fact, some people who beat on it. Rev. Reynolds. The ministers who signed the Manifesto. Gloria Floyd. Her grandmother who carried her to those Sunday meetings before she had to be carried. Hank Thomas and Charles Person, the Freedom Riders she had stood beside in 2021 as the master of ceremonies for the 60th anniversary of the bus burning. She had told them then, *Someone like me, standing in an official elected position, and because of you, I can do that.*

She was twenty-two when she said that. She is twenty-seven now. The math of the city's history is not abstract to her.

## The Record So Far

A year in, the things she has done are unglamorous. She negotiated the sale of the city-owned Regional Medical Center to Orlando Health, a 375-bed hospital and a five-year investment commitment, announced April 7, 2026. She raised starting salaries for police officers and firefighters in the FY 2025 budget. Her husband, Jamel Roston, is an officer in the Anniston Police Department, which she does not raise often, and which the people who run against her have not yet figured out how to use against her. She moved CDBG money into blight removal. She got the Barber Terrace public housing transformation moving. She put townhouses in South Anniston.

Her platform, when she announced for the seat in her own right, named four things: economic development, infrastructure, housing stability, and what she called "government transparency and public engagement," which in practice means town halls and explaining the budget to people who have never been asked to read one.

She said something at the candidacy launch that I keep going back to.

<PullQuote>
Leadership is not just about making decisions; it's about educating and involving the community every step of the way.
</PullQuote>

It is the kind of line that, in another mouth, would be a piece of furniture. From her it is a description of what her grandmother did with her at five.
