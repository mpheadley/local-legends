TELEPROMPTER — "Chief Ladiga, 1832"
Chief Ladiga Trail · Anniston to Piedmont, Alabama
Southern Legends · southernlegends.blog
---
READ PACE: Slow and direct. This is the trail that held the whole decade. Let it breathe.
---

I used to call it la-dee-ga.

I didn't know how else to say it. I also didn't know whose land I was running on.

My first time on the trail was in college. It ran right through the Jacksonville State campus, crossed Highway 204, and I had an apartment that joined it. I walked and ran it north toward Piedmont and back. I didn't think much about where I was.

Heather and I walked it when we were dating. That's partly how I got to know her outside the classroom.

---

When I came back to Alabama, I was appointed pastor at Weaver First United Methodist Church. The trail runs right through Weaver. The parsonage was an eighth of a mile from it.

I ran four or five miles on it a couple times a week — wrote sermons on it, brainstormed with Heather, played at Weaver Elwell Park. I'd take breaks from work just to go. Church members lived along it that we'd see as we passed.

I cried a lot on that trail, as I remember.

We taught all four kids to ride their bikes there. The kids called the section south of Elwell Park the tree tunnel — that stretch toward Michael Tucker Park where the canopy closes over you. We pushed the twins in the double stroller through it. I ran the Woodstock 5K with them in that stroller. Their little cheeks would bounce, and people would smile when we passed.

---

It was around 7:30 one summer morning, still cool, dew on everything and morning mist on the trail. Pink mimosa blooms hung over the path.

I was running the section north of the Weaver Lane crossing. Noah rode beside me on his little bike — five, maybe six years old.

He noticed the trash on the side of the trail.

"Why do people leave trash?" he asked.

  "Because they're broken."

---

We moved to the farm in April 2021, right there on the trail, right behind Walmart. The extension south of Michael Tucker Park had been promised for years — I'd tracked the project, downloaded the PDFs.

When they finally broke ground, we could watch the clearing from the property.

It was raining hard the day of the grand opening. Everyone moved inside the Coldwater Mountain Brew Pub — reporters, council members, cameras — while Ciara Smith gave the speech. She was vice mayor then, standing in for Toby Bennington, who'd been in a car accident and couldn't be there. Toby had been the spear on this project for fifteen years.

When the rain let up, they went back outside and cut the ribbon.

Four million dollars. Brush clearing, bridge rebuilding, the whole corridor.

Once the trail was paved, we'd ride our bikes half a mile north to Cedar Springs Creek and swim. Sunday nights, Sabbath eve — crossing the trail with the kids, my headlamp lighting the way to Walmart. Ice cream and beer runs.

---

I tried to build a bridge across the trail.

I'd also worked to run electricity from the Walmart parking lot pole, across the trail, to the farm. Alabama Power cut down a tree. The pole went in.

I never turned it on. By the time they were installing it, I was up the hill in my bedroom. Late September 2025. Hiding from the world.

The lumber for the bridge sat next to the trail shop site, eventually overgrown with weeds, until the house sold.

The bridge that never was. The power pole that was never turned on.

---

I ran it while I was manic.

I had whole conversations on that trail — starting at sunrise, hours through the day, walking north and south and back, talking into my phone. I was trying to think my way out of something you can't think your way out of. The mimosa blooms were there the whole time.

Around November 2024, I was meeting my dad weekly to ride the trail north from Michael Tucker Park — early in the morning. Him on his recumbent, me on the old Peugeot my father-in-law had given me. We'd talk about my recovery.

Once, near the end, I walked from downtown Anniston all the way back to the farm — about four miles, in my farm boots, depressed. The trail south of our property had just been finished. It was the first and only time I saw the whole thing completed.

---

What I know now that I didn't know then:

Chief Ladiga signed away his people's land in 1832. His people, the Muscogee Creek, were removed. The railroad was built on that corridor. The railroad was abandoned. Now there's a trail named after him.

The farmhouse we lived in was built in 1832. The same year. We had a copy of the deed. I remember being blown away when I made that connection — the same year Chief Ladiga signed the treaty, someone built the house I was living in.

Jacksonville, the town where I first ran that trail — Ladiga personally owned that land. He sold it in 1833 for two thousand dollars to a speculator named Charles White Peters. That land became the town. JSU sits on it.

  I was running Ladiga's land before I knew his name.

The trail ends in Piedmont at a shop called Chief Ladiga Trading Post — fudge, ice cream, and firearms.

---

During the manic episode, I had an idea for a campaign — a multi-year effort building toward the two hundredth anniversary of the treaty. The city of Anniston, the Muskogee Creek Nation, Rails to Trails, the farm. All of it connected. I pitched it to a t-shirt printer at the Jacksonville Farmers Market.

I felt like I was seeing something true.

2032 is six years away.

These are ideas I had when I was manic. I'm not sure they're dead.

My son Soren has been walking it with his friend. They live in an apartment south of the university coliseum, a few hundred yards east of the trail. He doesn't know all of this yet.

Maybe he will.
