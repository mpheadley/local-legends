**[WHAT PCO IS — 60 seconds]**

Planning Center Online is church operations software. Started in 2006, built by a worship pastor who needed to schedule musicians. It grew from there.

Today it's a suite — ten modules, each priced separately. The basics run about fifty dollars a month. Full stack for a mid-size church, you're looking at two hundred to five hundred a month depending on headcount and modules. They serve about sixty thousand churches, mostly Protestant, mostly mid-size, mostly English-speaking North America.

It does real things well. Scheduling volunteers. Managing a database of attendees. Processing giving. Running check-in for kids. Planning worship sets. These are not nothing.

But here's what PCO is not: it's not a ministry platform. It is operations infrastructure. That distinction matters more than most church leaders have had time to think through.

---

**[THE TEARDOWN — 3-4 minutes]**

**1. Against — the thing nobody says out loud.**

Most churches use Planning Center. That's the problem.

When every church runs on the same platform, you get operational uniformity. Same volunteer flows, same giving funnels, same check-in kiosks. Infrastructure shapes culture. PCO didn't design churches to look like each other — but identical infrastructure produces similar institutions over time.

Matt watched it happen. Slowly. Then all at once.

**2. Message — what PCO actually does.**

Planning Center manages church operations. It tracks who attended, who gave, who volunteered, and who is scheduled for Sunday.

That's the sentence. That is what it does. Not "connects people" or "builds community" or "empowers ministry leaders." It tracks and schedules. Accurately. Reliably. At scale. That's it.

**3. TAM — who they reach versus who they claim.**

They say they serve all churches. They actually serve churches with enough operational complexity to need what they built.

Small churches under 200 — they use maybe two modules. The price-per-module model hits them disproportionately. A church of 150 pays almost the same per capita as a church of 1,500.

Churches under 100, rural churches, bi-vocational pastors running lean — PCO is often too expensive, too complex, or both. That's a lot of churches. Most estimates put the number of US congregations under 100 at around seventy percent. PCO's real addressable market is narrower than their messaging suggests.

**4. Kill — the metric that ends them.**

Switching cost. That's what keeps churches on PCO. Fifteen years of giving records, volunteer history, a people database with notes going back to 2009 — you don't migrate that casually.

But here's what changes that: if a credible alternative offers data portability with a migration service, the switching cost collapses. Not for everyone at once. But the churches who are already frustrated — and there are plenty — they move.

If a competitor builds a one-click migration tool for PCO exports, Planning Center's retention number changes. That's the metric. Watch it.

**5. Moat — how real it is.**

Real. And partially cloneable.

The moat is switching cost plus integrations. CCLI, ProPresenter, Planning Center's own app ecosystem — it's woven into the production workflow of thousands of churches. That takes years to replicate.

What's cloneable: the feature set. None of what PCO does is technically hard. It's years of iteration and polish, but a well-funded team builds a comparable product in eighteen months. The integrations take longer. The trust takes longest.

Their moat is time and habit, not technology. Time and habit erode when something better shows up and makes switching easy enough.

**6. Failure Mode — what they're not watching.**

They're watching headcount and module adoption. They're not watching pastoral attrition.

PCO helps you manage the people who show up. It has no concept of the pastor. No sermon prep. No formation tracking. No way to know if the teaching is landing. No pastoral voice tool.

What that means over time: the platform serves the institution, not the person leading it. And when a pastor burns out — and they're burning out at record rates — PCO has no data on it. No signals. No early warning.

They're optimizing for operational retention. The pastoral failure mode is happening one congregation at a time and they're not measuring it.

**7. Content — their content strategy and where it breaks.**

PCO's content is tutorials and feature announcements. It lives in their help center, their YouTube channel, their blog.

Institution-facing. How to set up a workflow. How to run a volunteer background check. How to configure a giving form.

There is no pastoral voice content. No theology of ministry. No formation lens. No content that meets a pastor at 11pm on a Saturday when the sermon isn't ready.

Their content strategy treats ministry as an operation. That's fine — that's what they built. But it leaves an entire category of need unaddressed. Unaddressed in their content means unaddressed in their product.

**8. Distribution — their channel map and the gap in it.**

PCO grows through church staff networks. Associate pastors recommend it to senior pastors. Worship leaders tell their friends. It spreads laterally through denominational relationships and conference hallways.

It does not grow through pastors who are struggling. Struggling pastors don't go to conferences. They don't post recommendations. They go quiet.

The distribution gap is pastoral isolation. PCO has no product and no content strategy that reaches a pastor who is struggling in private. They grow through success networks, not through need networks.

That's where the opening is.

**[THE GAP — 30 seconds]**

PCO tells you who showed up. It cannot tell you if any of it mattered.

That's the gap. It's not a feature gap. It's a category gap. Operations and formation are not the same thing, and no module they build will cross that line. They were never trying to.

### Planning Center Online: An Honest Teardown by Someone Who Used It for Sixteen Years

I ran Planning Center Online for sixteen years across three congregations. First Baptist Muncie, Indiana. Weaver First UMC, Alabama. Anniston First UMC, Alabama. Every module. Both denominations.

This is not a competitor complaint. It's an honest assessment from someone who used the product the way it was meant to be used — and still found it missing the thing that mattered most.

---

### 1. Against: What Nobody Says About PCO

Planning Center is good at what it does. The problem is what it does.

When every church runs on the same operational platform, you get operational uniformity. Same volunteer flows, same giving funnels, same people database logic. Infrastructure shapes culture. PCO didn't design churches to look alike — but identical infrastructure produces similar institutions over time.

The harder truth: PCO treats your congregation as an audience to be managed. That's not a bug in their design. It's the design. Ministry operations and ministry formation are different categories, and PCO only covers one of them.

---

### 2. What PCO Actually Does

Planning Center manages church operations. It tracks who attended, who gave, who volunteered, and who is scheduled for next Sunday.

That's it. That's the sentence. Not "connects community" or "empowers pastors." It schedules and tracks — accurately and reliably. For that specific job, it's very good. The confusion comes when churches expect more.

---

### 3. Who They Actually Serve

PCO claims to serve all churches. In practice, they serve churches with enough operational complexity to justify their per-module pricing model.

A church of 150 people running five modules pays nearly the same per capita as a church of 1,500. Small churches, rural churches, and bi-vocational pastors — roughly seventy percent of US congregations fall under 100 attendees — get hit hardest by the pricing structure. PCO's real addressable market skews mid-size and up. Their messaging doesn't reflect that.

---

### 4. What Would End Them

Switching cost is PCO's retention engine. Fifteen years of giving records, volunteer history, pastoral notes from 2009 — you don't migrate that on a Tuesday.

One thing changes this: a credible alternative with data portability and a migration service. Not for every church. For the churches already frustrated. And there are more of those than PCO's NPS score captures.

The metric to watch: export-and-migrate rate. If a competitor makes switching easy enough, PCO's churn number moves. They know this. Watch whether they respond with data portability tools or with lock-in features. That answer will tell you everything about what they're afraid of.

---

### 5. How Real Their Moat Is

Real. And partially cloneable.

The moat is switching cost plus integrations — CCLI, ProPresenter, the broader church tech ecosystem that PCO has embedded itself into over two decades. That takes time to replicate.

What's cloneable: the feature set. Nothing PCO does is technically novel. The moat is time and habit, not technology. Time and habit erode when the switching cost drops far enough.

---

### 6. What They're Not Watching

PCO watches headcount and module adoption. They don't watch pastoral attrition.

The platform serves the institution. It has no concept of the pastor as a person with a formation life, a preaching arc, a burnout risk. No sermon prep. No formation tracking. No signal that the person leading the congregation is failing in private.

Pastoral burnout is accelerating across every denomination. PCO has no data on it because they never built for it. They're optimizing for operational retention while the pastoral failure mode compounds one congregation at a time.

---

### 7. Their Content Strategy

PCO's content is tutorial and feature-announcement content. Useful. Institution-facing. Completely silent on anything that happens between a pastor and a text on a Saturday night.

No theology of ministry. No formation lens. No pastoral voice content. Their content strategy treats ministry as an operation because their product does. That's internally consistent. It's also a wide open category.

---

### 8. Their Distribution Gap

PCO grows through church staff networks. Recommendations pass laterally through associate pastors, worship leaders, denominational relationships. It spreads through success networks.

It does not reach pastors who are struggling in private. Struggling pastors don't post software recommendations. They go quiet. PCO has no product and no distribution channel that reaches need networks instead of success networks.

That gap is where GCO and SermonCoach were built to enter.
