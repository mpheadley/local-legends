# PCO Teardown — Matt's Voice
*Read at conversational pace. ~5-7 min. B-roll cues in [brackets].*

---

Planning Center is not a ministry platform. It's a calendar with a database — and most churches don't know the difference.

---

Sixteen years. Every module. Three congregations. Two denominations.

I ran PCO at First Baptist Muncie, Indiana. Then Weaver First UMC in Alabama. Then Anniston First UMC. Services, People, Giving, Check-Ins, Groups, Registrations, Music Stand, Spaces. I know what it does. I know what it costs. And I know what it never touches.

That's why I'm here.

---

Planning Center Online is church operations software. Started in 2006, built by a worship pastor who needed to schedule musicians. It grew from there.

[B-ROLL: PCO homepage, module list]

Today it's a suite — ten modules, each priced separately. The basics run about fifty dollars a month. Full stack for a mid-size church, you're looking at two hundred to five hundred a month depending on headcount and modules. They serve about sixty thousand churches, mostly Protestant, mostly mid-size, mostly English-speaking North America.

It does real things well. Scheduling volunteers. Managing a database of attendees. Processing giving. Running check-in for kids. Planning worship sets. These are not nothing.

But here's what PCO is not: it's not a ministry platform. It is operations infrastructure. That distinction matters more than most church leaders have had time to think through.

---

**The thing nobody says out loud.**

Most churches use Planning Center. That's the problem.

When every church runs on the same platform, you get operational uniformity. Same volunteer flows, same giving funnels, same check-in kiosks. And then Sunday after Sunday, every church that uses PCO starts to feel like the same church.

PCO didn't design churches to look like each other. But it did give them identical infrastructure. And infrastructure shapes culture whether you intend it to or not. If your tech stack treats people as attendees to be scheduled and counted, your pastoral culture follows. Slowly. Then all at once.

I watched it happen.

---

**What it actually does.**

Planning Center manages church operations. It tracks who attended, who gave, who volunteered, and who is scheduled for Sunday.

That's the sentence. That is what it does. Not "connects people" or "builds community" or "empowers ministry leaders." It tracks and schedules. Accurately. Reliably. At scale. That's it.

---

**Who they actually serve.**

They say they serve all churches. They actually serve churches with enough operational complexity to need what they built.

[B-ROLL: PCO pricing page]

Small churches under 200 — they use maybe two modules. The price-per-module model hits them disproportionately. A church of 150 pays almost the same per capita as a church of 1,500. Most estimates put the number of US congregations under 100 at around seventy percent. PCO's real addressable market is narrower than their messaging suggests.

---

**What would end them.**

Switching cost. That's what keeps churches on PCO. Fifteen years of giving records, volunteer history, a people database with notes going back to 2009 — you don't migrate that casually.

One thing changes this: a credible alternative with data portability and a migration service. The churches who are already frustrated — and there are plenty — they move.

If a competitor builds a one-click migration tool for PCO exports, Planning Center's retention number changes. That's the metric to watch.

---

**What they're not watching.**

They're watching headcount and module adoption. They're not watching pastoral attrition.

PCO helps you manage the people who show up. It has no concept of the pastor. No sermon prep. No formation tracking. No way to know if the teaching is landing.

What that means over time: the platform serves the institution, not the person leading it. And when a pastor burns out — and they're burning out at record rates — PCO has no data on it. No signals. No early warning.

They're optimizing for operational retention. The pastoral failure mode is happening one congregation at a time and they're not measuring it.

---

**Their content gap.**

PCO's content is tutorials and feature announcements. How to set up a workflow. How to run a volunteer background check. How to configure a giving form.

No pastoral voice content. No theology of ministry. No formation lens. No content that meets a pastor at 11pm on a Saturday when the sermon isn't ready.

That's unaddressed in their content because it's unaddressed in their product. That gap is significant.

---

**Their distribution gap.**

[B-ROLL: church conference hallway, staff network graphic]

PCO grows through church staff networks. Associate pastors recommend it to senior pastors. Worship leaders tell their friends. It spreads through success networks.

It does not grow through pastors who are struggling. Struggling pastors don't go to conferences. They don't post recommendations. They go quiet.

The distribution gap is pastoral isolation. No product. No content. No channel that reaches a pastor who is struggling in private.

That's where the opening is.

---

PCO tells you who showed up. It cannot tell you if any of it mattered.

That's the gap. Not a feature gap. A category gap. Operations and formation are not the same thing, and no module they build will cross that line. They were never trying to.

---

I built SermonCoach and Gather Center Online because the gap isn't fixable from inside PCO.

[B-ROLL: SermonCoach app]

SermonCoach is sermon prep with an AI coaching layer. You bring the text, the congregation, the week — it pushes back on your structure, your clarity, your application. Not to write the sermon for you. To make the one you're already writing sharper.

[B-ROLL: GCO dashboard]

Gather Center Online connects that to formation tracking. Not attendance — formation. Are the people in your congregation growing? What are the patterns across your teaching calendar? What's working in small groups that isn't working on Sunday?

PCO knows if your volunteer showed up. GCO asks what your congregation learned last year.

Different product. Built for what they left out.
