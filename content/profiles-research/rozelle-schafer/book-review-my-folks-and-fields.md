# The Mail Carrier Who Wrote the Whole World Down
### On *Recollections: My Folks and Fields* by Eddie Bell Rozelle

I have read this book more than once, which is the first thing worth saying about it, because most books do not survive a second reading and this one gets better.

Eddie Bell Rozelle was born in 1890 at Hatchett Creek, in Clay County, Alabama — a place most people have never heard of and will never drive through on purpose. He went to Alabama Polytechnic. He taught in one-room schools in Clay County and then Talladega County for fifteen years. Then in 1927 he took a job carrying rural mail, and he carried it until 1963. Thirty-six years on the same roads. When he retired, he did the thing almost nobody does. He sat down and wrote the world he'd grown up in, before it was gone, and he did it himself — on a manual typewriter, run off on a mimeograph machine, bound in heavy paper in Talladega in 1960.

There was no publisher. There was no advance. There was a man who had decided that the way people lived in his valley — how they farmed, how they doctored, how they schooled the children, how they buried the dead and married the living and made an evening out of a singing — was worth setting down so it would not disappear.

That's the whole book, and that's why it matters.

It is not a beautiful book in the way we're taught to want books to be beautiful. It doesn't strain for a lyric. It tells you how sorghum was made and who made it. It tells you what a country doctor could and could not do. It tells you what it cost, in labor and in loss, to grow up on a hardscrabble farm before the roads were paved and before the government came out that far. Rozelle is not sentimental about it. He is not romantic about it. He remembers it plainly, which is the only honest way to remember a hard place you loved.

I keep coming back to it because it is the exact thing I am trying to do here, only he did it seventy years ahead of me and with a mimeograph. A man who stayed. Who paid attention his whole working life — a mail carrier sees everything, every house on every road, every year. And who understood, correctly, that if he didn't write it down, no one would, and the particular texture of a particular place would go under the ground with the last people who remembered it.

There is a second act to this book that I did not expect, and it is the part I can't stop thinking about.

The reason I can hold this book in my hands at all is that his daughter, Rebecca Rozelle Burt, brought it back into print. She was a JSU English professor. She spent a whole career teaching Alabama kids to read and write, and near the end of it she took her father's typewritten, mimeographed act of remembering and edited it into a new edition so it wouldn't be lost a second time. She wrote her own novel, too — *Facing Bulls*, set in a Talladega that isn't quite Talladega. She died in April of 2025.

So what you actually have, if you buy this book, is two acts of the same devotion, one generation apart. A father who wrote his world down so his people wouldn't vanish. A daughter who spent her life on words and then made sure her father's words made it into the next century. Neither of them was famous. Both of them stayed. Both of them understood that a place is only kept alive by somebody who decides to keep it.

That is the entire argument of Southern Legends, and I didn't have to make it up. It was already written, in Clay County, on a typewriter, by a man who carried the mail.

Read it. It's [in print](https://www.amazon.com/RECOLLECTIONS-FIELDS-Eddie-B-Rozelle/dp/1514450046) — three dollars and change for the Kindle, twenty for the paperback. Buy the paperback. Some books you should be able to hold.
