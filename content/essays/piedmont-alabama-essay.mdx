I was sitting in a theater in Piedmont, Alabama watching seventh graders do *Wizard of Oz* when I realized something important.

Donald Miller's *A Million Miles in a Thousand Years* argues that you are the hero of your story. That the story gets better when you start living like the protagonist. That you need a guide — your Gandalf, your Yoda — who helps you on the journey and then steps back when you arrive.

The framework sells. It has sold very well.

But sitting in that theater, watching three kids play the Scarecrow and the Tinman and the Lion, I saw something Miller's framework didn't account for. Those three are not sidekicks to Dorothy's hero. They are three broken companions who become each other's guide. The Scarecrow thinks he has no brain. The Tinman thinks he has no heart. The Lion thinks he has no courage. They are each other's witness and each other's mirror and each other's proof that the thing they're looking for was already there.

That is not a hero's journey. That is a community.

Jason Wright put those kids on that stage. He had been teaching music in Piedmont for years — building voices, running rehearsals, taking seventh graders seriously enough to give them Shakespeare and Sondheim and Baum. He taught them the songs. He put them in front of an audience. And the audience, which included me, learned something we hadn't paid admission to learn.

## Piedmont, Alabama

Piedmont is a small city in Calhoun County at the edge of the Talladega National Forest, population five thousand. It sits at the foot of the Cheaha Mountain range — not quite in the mountains but near enough that the topography shows up in the skyline every morning.

The city's industrial history runs through the twentieth century: textiles, manufacturing, the rhythms of a small Alabama city whose economic base has shifted more than once and is shifting still. The chief employer mix has changed. What has not changed is the particular character of a place that is small enough that everyone knows what the theater program is doing this year and whether the school choir sounded good at the Christmas concert.

The Chief Ladiga Trail runs from Anniston to Piedmont — thirty-three miles of rail-trail through the Calhoun County landscape. The trail is one of the longest rail-trails in Alabama. It ends, or begins, in Piedmont. You can walk from the Anniston museum district to the Piedmont trailhead in a day if you start early.

## The Theater

Piedmont has a theater tradition because Piedmont had people who believed that small-town Alabama kids deserved to be on stage. Jason Wright was one of those people. His students have gone on to perform with CAST in Anniston, with CharACTers in Gadsden, with the Theatre of Gadsden. Some of them have now auditioned for the same roles their teacher is playing.

That is the specific metric of a good teacher: when the students are auditioning alongside you, and the competition is real, and you are proud of it.

## What the Seventh Graders Knew

The Scarecrow gets a diploma at the end. The Tinman gets a heart-shaped clock. The Lion gets a medal. None of these objects actually give them what they thought they were missing. What they actually got was the road — the time together, the danger shared, the fact of having carried each other.

The kids in Piedmont who played those parts knew this better than they knew they knew it. They had spent months in rehearsal, working through the blocking and the harmonies and the nerves, depending on each other to come in on time, holding each other accountable when they didn't. By the time they were on stage, they had already done the thing the play was about.

I went in expecting a school production. I came out with a different framework for thinking about community, about guides, about what it actually means to walk with someone through something hard.

Piedmont gave me that. A seventh-grade theater program in a small Calhoun County city gave me a way of seeing that I have been writing from ever since.
