The first church I remember is First Baptist Anniston, which is a way of saying I was formed by a particular flavor of evangelical before I knew what evangelical meant. Bulletins. Suits. A choir loft that was not actually used for the choir. The Lord's Supper served in individual plastic cups. I didn't know this was a choice. I thought it was just how it went.

It is, in fact, one of eight ways it goes — and those eight ways shape people differently.

---

There is a word for what I'm describing: spiritual stream. Not denomination. Not tribe. A deeper current running underneath the surface of your Sunday morning, underneath the particular arguments your church has had about the worship leader's choice of songs. Scholars have named them differently — Dallas Willard, Phyllis Tickle, Richard Foster all had their own taxonomies. They all agreed on roughly this: people encounter the sacred in different modes, and those modes leave different marks.

The contemplative stream prizes silence and presence. You know it by what it produces: people who are genuinely unhurried, who don't fill every silence, who seem to be reading the room at a frequency the rest of us can't quite catch. You find them in Quaker meetinghouses, in the centering prayer circles that meet in Methodist fellowship halls on Tuesday mornings, in people who went to Camp Lee on a retreat and never fully came back from what they heard in the silence there.

Dale Clem used to run the Academy for Spiritual Formation out of Camp Lee. Dale is a man whose formation shows up in his posture — not hunched, not performed, just quiet in a way that isn't absence. His conference introduced me to the idea that there was a contemplative stream within my own tradition. I had been in the church my whole life and nobody had mentioned it. They hadn't mentioned Merton. They hadn't mentioned Rohr. They hadn't mentioned the possibility that your prayer life could be less about talking and more about listening.

That retreat changed my anthropology.

---

Northeast Alabama has a formation geography. If you grew up here, you got shaped by it whether you recognized it or not.

The charismatic stream runs strong in the Pentecostal churches scattered along the county roads — the ones with the hand-lettered signs, the ones that still have revival weeks in July, the ones where somebody gets the gift of tongues on a Wednesday night and the whole church knows by Thursday. This stream prizes experience. Encounter. The sense that God is active, present, and demonstrably so. People formed in this stream often have an immediacy to their faith that can look unserious from outside and look like the whole thing from inside.

The justice stream runs through the historically Black churches that have been the backbone of community organizing in Calhoun County since before anyone called it community organizing. These are churches where the sermon has always had to do two things at once: tend the soul and tell the truth about the world. Formation in this stream produces people who cannot separate personal faith from public consequence. They always looked at me a little sideways when I described my practice as contemplative. Not wrong. Just incomplete, their eyes said.

The evangelical stream — the one I was handed at First Baptist, the one that still runs deepest in the soil of NE Alabama — prizes the text. The authority of Scripture, the necessity of conversion, the legibility of salvation. It is not wrong about the text. It sometimes struggles with what to do when the text doesn't answer the question you brought, or when the question feels too expensive to ask. But it produces people who know their Bible, and that is not a small thing.

---

The strange thing about living in a place long enough is that you absorb more than one stream.

I was formed evangelical. Then I spent fifteen years in United Methodist churches and absorbed a liturgical sensibility I didn't know I was missing — the weight of the Table, the rhythm of the Christian year, the idea that your body is part of your prayer and not just a vehicle for transporting your brain to the sanctuary. Then Dale Clem and the Academy handed me a contemplative practice, and I realized I had been swimming upstream my whole adult life when I could have been reading the current.

Now I use the Enneagram, the Big Five, the Jungian types, the contemplative tradition's map of desire and wound — not because I think any single framework is the whole thing, but because I think the streams braid. The evangelical in me still believes the text matters. The contemplative in me still needs silence. The justice strand, learned slowly from people whose tradition runs older than mine in this county, still asks what my practice is for.

What I know now is that I was not just formed by doctrine. I was formed by place. By the specific people who occupied the specific buildings on the specific roads that shaped the first thirty years of my life. Formation is not abstract. It has a zip code.

---

If you grew up in NE Alabama, your stream has a name. You probably haven't named it.

The name doesn't unlock a secret. It doesn't tell you anything you didn't already carry. But there is something that happens when you put a name to the current you've been swimming in — you can finally see the current. You can ask whether it's going where you want to go. You can notice where another stream has been braiding in for years without your permission.

That's all the quiz is. A mirror, not a verdict.
