Oxford, Alabama sits at the intersection of US-78 and Interstate 20. Everything else follows from that.

The commercial spine of Calhoun County runs along Quintard Drive — the restaurants, the retailers, the gas stations, the superstores. When the county wants something, it drives to Oxford. The county has been doing this for decades. Oxford built its identity around being the place you drive to, and it has been very good at it.

---

## What Oxford Is

Oxford is not Anniston, and it does not try to be.

Anniston has Noble Street, the museums, the history, the civic weight of a city that was built to be something. Oxford has Quintard Drive, and Quintard Drive has everything you need. It is practical in a way that some cities manage to make feel like a virtue.

Oxford Lake sits at the city's center — a 122-acre reservoir turned public park, with walking trails, a fishing pier, and a pavilion. It is where Oxford's residents actually go when they are not buying something. On Saturday mornings in the warmer months it feels like the whole city is circling the water.

The Oxford Civic Center and Oxford Performing Arts Center anchor the city's cultural presence. CAST Alabama — the community theater company — has staged productions at the Oxford Performing Arts Center. Garfrerick's Café and Catering, one of the county's landmark caterers, operates out of Oxford. The city is not without its places that matter.

---

## The Quintard Corridor

The strip along Quintard Drive is Calhoun County's commercial geography made visible. Every major national chain that operates in the county operates here. Every regional institution that needed room to expand found it on this corridor.

This is not a failure of vision. It is the result of a city that understood what it was and built for it. The highway interchange is the most reliable economic asset a small city in the American South can have, and Oxford used it.

The county's commercial density along this corridor means that Oxford's tax base has historically been healthier than many cities its size. The tradeoff is a city whose public face is a commercial strip — which is to say, whose public face looks like everywhere. The identity is real; it just requires going deeper than Quintard Drive to find it.

## Lickskillet

The community known as Lickskillet sits in the eastern edges of what is now Oxford, along the old Choccolocco Road corridor. The name predates the city's incorporation, predates most of the commercial development, and belongs to an older layer of the county's geography — the rural crossroads community names that survived the twentieth century without being polished into something more presentable.

Lickskillet is the bottom of the pot. It is what you get when the good stuff has been served and you scrape what's left. As a place name it is honest in a way that most Alabama place names try not to be.

---

## What Comes Next

Oxford in 2026 is a city at a quiet inflection point. The commercial strip remains, but the question of what Oxford is *besides* the commercial strip is getting more interesting. The Oxford Lake corridor is being invested in. The arts institutions are growing. Businesses like Garfrerick's and Romarin et Corbeaux — which moved from Oxford back to Noble Street in Anniston after threats — show that the county's independent commercial culture is not uniformly heading toward the chain model.

Oxford built itself around what the highway delivered. The interesting question now is what it builds next.
