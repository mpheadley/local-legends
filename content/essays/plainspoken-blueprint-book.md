I've been working on something for a while now, and I want to tell you about it before it's finished.

A few years back I was sitting across from a guy who made custom furniture. Beautiful stuff. He'd been in business for eleven years and couldn't figure out why he kept losing jobs to people whose work wasn't as good. We sat at his workbench for about an hour. He told me about joinery. He told me about wood sourcing. He told me about the difference between a mortise and a tenon and why it mattered. I nodded. I didn't know what a tenon was.

I asked him what he told people when they asked what he did.

He said, "I create heirloom-quality artisan furniture using traditional joinery techniques and sustainably sourced hardwoods."

I said, "What if you just said you build furniture that gets passed down?"

He looked at me for a second. Then he said, "That's it."

That's the whole book.

I've been working with small business owners long enough to know that the problem is almost never the product. The product is usually good. Sometimes it's great. The problem is that the owner is too close to it. They know too much. And when they open their mouth to explain what they do, what comes out is everything they know instead of the one thing the customer needs to hear.

Clever confuses. Clarity sells.

That's the tagline for the book I'm finishing now. It's called Plainspoken Blueprint. It's a messaging book for small business owners who keep getting in their own way with their pitch. I built it from conversations with founders who couldn't close a client not because their offer was weak but because their words were working against them.

The book walks through eight steps — from identifying the customer's actual problem to building a message that makes someone say yes. It's not a marketing textbook. It's not a branding course. It's closer to what I wish someone had handed me when I was trying to explain what I was building and couldn't get a straight sentence out.

Part of what I know about this I learned the hard way. I've had to start over. I've had to explain myself from scratch to people who had no reason to trust me yet. You can't afford to be clever when you're starting over. You just need someone to understand you.

That turned out to be the whole lesson.

I'm writing this for Southern Legends because this community is full of people building things — shops, farms, studios, services, organizations. People who are good at what they do and don't always know how to say it. If that's you, or if you know someone like that, this book is for them.

I'll share more as I get closer to finishing.

Matt
