Wade Allen picked me up in Muncie every Monday and we drove south to Indianapolis together.

Wade was my boss. He was the senior pastor at First Baptist, and I was the worship arts director. We held our staff meeting in the car, which meant the meeting was over when we hit Indianapolis, and then I got out and walked to the Megabus stop alone.

The Muncie-to-Indianapolis leg was mine and Wade's. The Indianapolis-to-Chicago leg was mine and a white double-decker bus and a tablet of Dramamine.

On Wednesday I did the whole thing in reverse. Metra from Lombard to Union Station. Megabus south. Heather picked me up at the Indianapolis stop and drove me back to Muncie, because I had to be at band rehearsal by evening. Monday out, Wednesday back. For three years.

I would take half a pill, find a window seat, open a book, and be asleep by Crawfordsville. When I woke it was later than I thought. The paper on my lap — social ethics, always unfinished — had gone from white to gray in the light, and the window was full of flat Illinois going dark, the fields the color of a bruise, one water tower, then nothing, then another. The Megabus cost about twenty dollars each way. Northern Seminary cost something I do not want to calculate now.

I once tried to add up the hours I spent on that bus. It came to more than a month of my life. Accumulated. If you stacked all those seats end to end they would fill thirty-some days. I don't know if that's a lot. I know it's a number. The number is: every week for three years, you get on the bus.

---

We were newlyweds in Muncie. First a trailer, then our first house. Before the kids, before any of the weight that would come, Heather and I walked the sidewalks. Flat Indiana streets, hours of them. Some of the best conversations we have ever had happened there, on concrete, going nowhere. I was being trained in two things at once that year and I only knew about one of them.

---

Union Station in Chicago sits on the river. You walk up from underground and you are standing in a cathedral no denomination built. Marble floors. Arched ceilings. Light falling through high windows onto travelers who are not looking at each other. I was one of them. I walked through without looking up. I had a train to catch.

The Metra goes west. Naperville, Lisle, Lombard. I rode it with my backpack and my theology textbooks and the particular exhaustion of a man in his early thirties trying to hold down a ministry job and a marriage and a graduate degree in the gaps between them.

At Lombard I walked three blocks north from the station to the parking lot of the Lombard United Methodist Church.

My brother Justin's truck was there. A 1989 Ford Ranger, navy, five-speed manual. Justin had let me keep it. I left it in that church lot all week, every week, while I was back in Indiana doing ministry.

The man who plowed the lot did not appreciate this.

I don't know his name. I know his opinion of my truck. He expressed it by burying it. Deliberately, efficiently, every week it sat there past Sunday. I'd ride in on Wednesday and find the Ranger encased. Not snowed on. Buried. The plow had pushed everything in the lot into a wall around it.

I dug it out with my hands. Illinois winters, no gloves, seminary books under one arm. I dug out that truck and climbed in and eased it into first and drove to class.

I think about that man sometimes. He was not wrong. I was parking a truck in a church lot all week without asking. That is a fair grievance. What I remember is not the injustice. What I remember is that you dig it out. You don't get to leave it there. You show up, you dig, you go.

That's the thing about a truck your brother lends you. You don't own it. You take care of it. You give it back.

---

Coach Wayne Gordon ran Lawndale Community Church on the west side of Chicago. He used to say — and I heard this in a classroom, and I wrote it in the margin of my notebook and circled it — "Anything worth doing is worth doing poorly."

He did not mean do bad work. He meant start. Build the crappy gym with the secondhand equipment in the neighborhood nobody is investing in. The athletes will come. The leaders will grow. The gym will get better. But first you build it badly, because that is the only way it gets built at all.

I was at Northern Seminary trying to figure out what I was supposed to do. I thought I was being trained. I did not understand that I was already doing it. The bus was the training. The Dramamine was the training. Getting on a forty-dollar round trip every week when we did not have forty extra dollars — that was formation. My professor Dave Fitch used the phrase "faithful presence." You show up in a place and you stay and you let the place change you. I thought the place was the classroom. The place was the commute.

---

One winter I was standing at a bus stop downtown, I don't remember which one, and a man was walking the crowd asking people something. This was not unusual. People worked that crowd of travelers, and by then I had learned how to relate to it. That is the honest part. I had been making this trip for over a year. I had gotten good at it. I could tune a person out without breaking stride, without taking my headphones out, the way the people who actually lived in the city did it. I had practiced. I was fluent.

So I gave him the standard line before he could finish. I'm sorry, I don't have anything.

He stopped. His face changed.

The least you could do is listen to me, man. I was trying to ask you a question.

He kept walking.

I took my headphones out. My heart did the thing it does when you have done something small and wrong and you can't undo it. He was gone. I had been in seminary for over a year by then. Reading about faithful presence. Writing papers on showing up in a neighborhood no one is investing in. And I had my headphones in.

I used that moment in a sermon two years later. It preached well. I don't know his name. I don't know what his question was.

---

I graduated in June 2013. The ceremony was out in Elmhurst. My family drove up — Heather, and Hannah who was two, and the smallest visible curve under Heather's dress that was Noah or Soren or Sage, we did not know yet which one. Our friend Christina came. We took pictures outside. My face in those pictures is the face of a man who has slept about six hours a week for three years, which is about right, and who has also done a thing he is proud of.

We drove back to Indiana. I handed Justin his keys.

---

I'm back in Alabama now. Have been for a long time.

The things I learned in Lombard I could not have learned here. Not because Alabama is lesser. Because I wasn't finished yet. Some things you can't learn at home. You have to leave, and then you have to come back.

The Wayne Gordon rule is in everything I build now. I ship things before they're ready. I build crappy gyms. I make the commute even when it costs money I don't have, because the commute was never the thing you endure on the way to the thing. The commute was the thing.

When people ask me where I'm from, I say Alabama. That's true. I'm also from a trailer in Muncie, and a bus seat between Indianapolis and Chicago, and a navy Ford Ranger with a soft clutch parked at a Methodist church three blocks off the Metra line.

Somewhere in Lombard there is still a lot that gets plowed every winter. A man pushes the snow into a wall. There's no truck in it now.
