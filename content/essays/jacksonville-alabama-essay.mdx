I know the exact size of the stage at the Ernest Stone Performing Arts Center at Jacksonville State University. I know how the acoustics shift when the house is full versus half-empty. I know the weight of a prop razor in your hand when you are playing a man who has lost everything and found it again in the wrong thing.

In January 2007, I sang the Father in Humperdinck's *Hansel and Gretel* on that stage. In February 2009, I played Sweeney Todd — the demon barber of Fleet Street, the man who came home broken and couldn't find his way back. Jeremy Stovall conducted. Teresa Stricklin was music director. Eric Traynor directed. The *Gadsden Times* ran a preview: "Matt Headley stars in the title role."

That is one version of Jacksonville. The version where you are twenty-three and the songs go where they're supposed to go and you are in it completely and nothing outside the theater matters for a few hours.

## What Jacksonville Is

Jacksonville, Alabama is a college town. Jacksonville State University — founded 1883, eleven thousand students, Division I athletics, a music department that has sent people into churches and concert halls and school auditoriums across the state — sits at the center of the city and organizes everything around it.

The university is not incidental to the city. It is the city's reason for being in its current form. Without JSU, Jacksonville is a Calhoun County crossroads town. With it, Jacksonville is a place where young people from across Alabama and beyond come to become something, study something, meet someone. Some of them leave. Some of them stay. The ones who stay are usually the ones who found something here beyond the degree.

Jean Ellison and I were in the JSU Music Department at the same time. She has said, in a quote I used in the profile I wrote about her: *"We were at Jacksonville State Music Department together when the world felt like it was still in front of us."* That is the specific sensation of a college music department in a small Alabama town. The world is in front of you. The stage is right there. The ensemble is about to start.

## The Town Around the University

Jacksonville the town is quieter than the campus. Pizza places and coffee shops on Pelham Road. A community pool with posted hours. A pocket park behind the Italian restaurant where volunteers grow food in raised beds. The Jacksonville Community Garden of "Eatin" — the apostrophe and the dropped letter are exactly right for what it is — sits behind Roma's Italian on what used to be bare rock and clay.

The First United Methodist Church of Jacksonville is on Gayle Street. I was temporarily on staff there in 2022 as worship leader. The arrangement was what it was — a season of rebuilding, a chance to be useful, a particular kind of employment that is not really employment but is also not nothing.

## The Fence

There is a specific sentence I have written about Jacksonville that keeps coming back to me. It is from an essay about the wound being the credential — about why the guide who has descended and come back knows something the one who never went down there cannot know.

*You cannot describe what it felt like to paint someone else's fence in Jacksonville, Alabama in the summer while you were praying your farm didn't fail. That detail either lives in you or it doesn't.*

I was painting a fence in Jacksonville while my farm was dying. Not a metaphor. I had a paint roller and someone else's fence and too much time to think about whether the flower farm I had built with Heather was going to survive the summer. It didn't, eventually. The fence got painted.

Jacksonville was the place I went when things were hard, before and after the university years. The stage at the Stone Center was the place I sang when singing was the thing I needed. The town has held several different versions of me, and I am grateful to it for all of them.

## The People of Jacksonville

Jacksonville's permanent population — the people who didn't come for the university, or who came and stayed — includes the families who have been in Calhoun County for generations and the ones who arrived and put roots down in the town itself. Four Mile United Methodist Church sits just outside the city, one of the county's older rural congregations. The community garden draws people who want to grow something in the particular soil of this particular place.

Jay Robinson runs the Wesley Foundation at JSU — the Methodist campus ministry that has been sitting at the edge of college campuses since before most of the denominations that fund it were founded. He came up in Jacksonville, in the UMC tradition, and stayed. The Wesley house is the kind of institution that exists at the intersection of the university and the town — serving the students who flow through and the community that remains.
