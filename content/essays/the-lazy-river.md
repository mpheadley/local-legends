The lazy river is the best part of the whitewater park. Kids don't believe you when you tell them that. They want the slides, the drops, the big noise. The lazy river looks like giving up.

It isn't.

You can fight a lazy river. You can swim upstream, burn your arms out, get exactly nowhere, and tell yourself you're doing something. You can bail out, find a chair in the shade, call it rest. Both moves share an assumption: that what you're looking for is somewhere other than here.

I floated that river last Friday and caught myself doing something I don't do very often. I wasn't thinking about what comes next. I wasn't scanning anything. I was reading the water — where it curved, where it slowed, where the current wanted to go. And I was going with it.

That's the contemplative life. The mystics have been trying to describe this for a thousand years in language that sounds strange to most of us: surrender, attentiveness, abandon, presence. What they're pointing at looks less like a silent room and more like a man floating a river on a Friday afternoon, paying attention to what's actually here instead of what he thinks should be here.

Most people I work with are treating their lives like Six Flags. More options, more noise, more movement. The answer always feels like more. More strategy, more hustle, more optimization. More.

But the people who make it through hard seasons — the business owner who finally found her message, the pastor who stopped burning out every three years, the couple who stopped having the same fight in different rooms — they don't tell me they found their way by adding something. They tell me they stopped fighting something they were never meant to control. They learned to read the water.

That's what I do. I sit with people — business owners, faith leaders, couples, people in the middle of something they can't name yet — and I help them read their own water. Not to hand them a map. Not to tell them where the current should go. To help them pay attention to what's actually moving underneath the noise, before they decide what to do about it.

I used to call it spiritual direction. I've stopped. A director implies someone at the front of the room with a plan and a pointer. What I actually do is closer to floating alongside someone who has been swimming hard for a long time. You don't need direction. You need someone who knows what the current feels like, and will stay in the water with you while you figure it out.

The contemplative path isn't escape. It never was. Escape is just a faster way of ending up in the same spot, with a vacation attached. What the old traditions are pointing at is attention — the specific, unhurried practice of noticing what's actually here before you decide what to do about it. The noise doesn't go away. The river doesn't stop. But there is a difference between a person drowning in it and a person who has learned to read it. That difference isn't talent. It's practice. It's the willingness to stop swimming upstream long enough to feel which way the current is actually going.
