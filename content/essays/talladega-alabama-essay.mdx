Talladega, Alabama has a name that most people know for one thing.

The Superspeedway opened in 1969 and immediately became the fastest track in NASCAR — two and a half miles of banked tri-oval, speeds that regularly exceed two hundred miles per hour, crashes that people still reference by year like weather events. The Talladega 500 in October draws crowds that briefly make the city one of the most populated places in Alabama. Then the race ends and the crowd disperses and Talladega goes back to being itself.

What Talladega is, the rest of the year, is worth knowing.

---

## Talladega College

Talladega College was founded in 1867 by two formerly enslaved men — William Savery and Thomas Tarrant — alongside American Missionary Association missionaries. It was founded two years after the Civil War ended, on land donated by a former slaveholder. It is the oldest private HBCU in Alabama.

The College's Swayne Hall, built in 1852, predates the institution itself — it was a boys' school for white children before emancipation, and it became part of the campus when the College was founded. The Amistad Murals — six panels painted by Hale Woodruff in 1939 depicting the Amistad slave ship revolt — hang in the College's Savery Library. They are among the most significant works of African American art in Alabama, and they are in Talladega, not in a major metropolitan museum.

The Superspeedway is twenty miles outside the city. The Amistad Murals are in the library on the campus downtown.

## The Cheaha District

I sat in a meeting in Talladega in March 2016 — a District Charge Teleconference for the Cheaha District of the United Methodist Church. The kind of meeting where you talk about the upcoming annual conference, the pre-conference worship service, who is organizing what. I was a pastor in the district. Amy and I were being asked to add congregational singing to the opening gathering. Scott Johnson from Heflin was involved. We were organizing mass choir for the following year's pre-conference gathering.

That is the version of Talladega I know from the inside: a district meeting room, a planning conversation, the particular bureaucratic texture of denominational work. A meeting that has nothing to do with racing.

The Cheaha District covered Calhoun and surrounding counties — the same geography where I had been pastoring for years, the same landscape of small UMC churches and crossroads communities. Talladega was the anchor of the southern edge of that territory. First United Methodist Talladega anchored the church life the way Talladega College anchored the educational life and the Superspeedway anchored the economic life. Three different institutions, three different communities, all in the same small city.

## The City Itself

Talladega is the seat of Talladega County, population about fifteen thousand in the city proper. It has a historic downtown that is doing the work of preservation that small Southern cities do — some buildings restored, others waiting, a Main Street program trying to close the gap between what is and what was.

Talladega has oak trees that are old enough to have witnessed everything the city has been through: the founding, the college, the track, the industry that arrived and the industry that left. The landscape around the city — Talladega National Forest, the Chinnabee Silent Trail, Cheaha State Park — is some of the most beautiful in Alabama. The highest point in the state, Cheaha Mountain, is in Talladega County.

The city lives in the shadow of its most famous landmark in a way that is not entirely comfortable. The Superspeedway matters economically and in terms of name recognition. What the city has worked to articulate is that the name recognition doesn't exhaust the city.

Talladega College, the murals, the forest, the court square, the people who have been here since before the track was built — these are the other Talladega, the one that was there before 1969 and will be there after the last lap.
