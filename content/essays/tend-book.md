I've been officiating weddings for a long time. Long enough to know that most of the couples sitting across from me in premarital counseling are thinking about the ceremony. The flowers. Who's sitting where. Whether the caterer confirmed.

Not many of them are thinking about what they're bringing in.

That's not a criticism. It's just true. The year before a wedding is loud. There's a lot to plan and a lot of people with opinions about the planning. The inner work is quiet. It doesn't have a deadline. Nobody sends you a reminder about it.

Heather and I got married at the JSU Alumni House in Jacksonville. Summer. I remember her laughing at something I said during the vows. What I don't remember is anyone sitting us down and asking us the hard questions. Not the therapy questions — just the real ones. What does money mean to each of you? What did conflict look like in the house you grew up in? What do you expect from intimacy, and have you said that out loud? What does faith mean to you, and does it mean the same thing to your partner?

We figured it out. Most couples do. But figuring it out after the wedding is harder than it had to be.

I've spent the last several years thinking about that gap — between the wedding and the marriage. Between the celebration and the formation. I'm a pastor. I've done a lot of premarital counseling. I'm also Prepare/Enrich certified, which means I've had a framework for these conversations. What I didn't have was something I could hand a couple and say: work through this together, before you get here.

So I wrote it.

The book is called Tend: Before the Wedding. It's a workbook for the year before the wedding. Not a wedding planning book. There are plenty of those. This is about the people doing the planning — who they are, what they're carrying, what they expect, and whether they've said any of it out loud.

It covers five things: what you're bringing into the marriage from your own history; money and how you actually talk about it; conflict and what you do when it comes; intimacy and what that word means to each of you; and faith, whether you share it and what to do if you don't.

It's not a therapy workbook. I'm not a therapist. It's a companion — something to sit with, come back to, argue over a little. Something that asks the questions a good friend would ask if a good friend knew what to ask.

I'll be sharing more about it here over the coming months — pieces from the book, the questions behind the questions, some of what Heather and I have learned in our own marriage that I wish someone had handed us in a workbook the year before our wedding.

If you're engaged, or you know someone who is, keep an eye out. If you're a pastor or a counselor and this sounds like something your couples need, I'd love to talk.
