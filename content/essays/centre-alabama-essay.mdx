Cherokee County is the northeasternmost county in Alabama, and Centre is its seat.

The county is named for the people who were removed from it. The Cherokee had been in this territory for centuries — the mountain ridges, the river valleys, the routes through the Appalachian foothills that became the county roads and then the state highways. The county was organized in 1836, four years after the Creek removal and two years before the Trail of Tears took the remaining Cherokee west. It was named Cherokee County as the Cherokee were leaving.

That is not an unusual thing in Alabama. Half the counties carry the names of the people who were pushed out to make room for them. What is unusual is that you can still feel the shape of the old territory in the landscape — the ridges and the rivers run the way they ran before the lines were drawn.

---

## The Town

Centre proper is a small city of around three thousand people. It has the Coosa River running along its edge and the Lake Weiss reservoir — formed when the Coosa was dammed in the 1960s — stretching north into Georgia. The lake changed the character of the county considerably: it brought fishing and recreation and second homes and the particular economy of places where people from cities come to slow down.

The town square, the courthouse, the old storefronts along the main street — Centre has the bones of a county seat that has been a county seat for a long time. The Cherokee County courthouse is the institutional center of a county that runs from the Georgia line south toward Gadsden, from the Tennessee Valley down through the ridge country.

Little River Canyon cuts through the county's eastern edge — forty miles of canyon carved by the Little River, which runs along the top of Lookout Mountain before dropping into the valley. The canyon is managed by the National Park Service and is one of the deepest canyons east of the Mississippi. People drive past Centre to get to the canyon and sometimes stop in town and sometimes don't.

---

## What the County Does

Cherokee County's economy has never been simple. Agriculture, textiles, some manufacturing. The lake brought tourism. The canyon brings hikers and photographers and people from the cities who have read something about it.

Centre holds the county together in the way that county seats do — the courthouse, the school board, the hospital, the services that require a central location. It is a town defined largely by its administrative function, which sounds diminishing but is not. The county seat is the place where things get recorded, where disputes get resolved, where the formal record of the community's life is kept. Centre has been keeping that record for nearly two hundred years.

---

## The Name

Centre is spelled the British way — an affectation or a historical accident, depending on who you ask. It is one of the few Alabama cities where the colonial spelling survived the standardization push of the twentieth century. The name itself is simply geographic: the town was near the center of the county when it was established as county seat.

The county is named for a people. The seat is named for a location. There is a particular Alabama logic in that combination — the grand historical naming and the plainspoken practical one sitting next to each other without any apparent awareness of the tension.

---

## On the Coosa

The Coosa River runs through Cherokee County on its way south to Gadsden and then to the Tallapoosa confluence and eventually the Mobile Bay. The river has been running through this landscape since before anyone who now lives in Alabama arrived. The Cherokee called it something before the English named it Coosa.

The river is the oldest thing in Centre. The courthouse is old. The county is old. The river doesn't notice any of it.
