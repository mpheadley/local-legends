I've been working on something I want to tell you about.

For the past year, the Southern Legends profiles have been doing double work. On the surface, they're portraits of people in Calhoun County — the mushroom farmer at the downtown market, the trail that runs along an old rail bed, the man who shows up wherever the county needs someone to show up. But underneath, they've been something else. Research, maybe. Or reconnaissance. I've been learning how to write about a place I grew up in and left and lost and came back to, and I've been doing it by writing around it first.

The memoir is called Broken Ground.

It's about Pleasant Valley, Alabama. The valley itself — the way the ridges hold the weather in, the way the creek sounds different in winter. The church where I grew up, where my grandfather preached, where I eventually stood at the front and did the same. The farm Heather and I tried to build. The manic episode that took most of what I thought I was. The psychiatric hospital. The slow, unglamorous work of getting stable and learning to be a person again.

I want to be plain about what this book is not. It's not a recovery story. Recovery stories have arcs. They move from broken to fixed. That's not what happened to me, and it's not what I'm writing. What I'm writing is a turning story — about how the place I spent years trying to leave was trying to tell me something I wasn't ready to hear. I had to lose enough to get quiet enough to hear it.

I grew up in that valley and felt it was too small. I left for college, seminary, ministry. I came back to farm and felt the ground push back. Then I broke, and the hospital, and the slow walk back toward something. And the valley was still there. The ridge was still there. The creek was still there.

I'm not sure the place cared that I'd been gone.

Every Southern Legends profile is a fragment of this. When I sit across from someone in Anniston or Weaver or Jacksonville and ask them about their work, what I'm really asking is how a person makes a life in a particular place. How they stay. How the place shapes them when they're not paying attention. I've been asking that question because I'm still answering it for myself.

I don't have a publication date. I don't have an agent. I have a manuscript I've been building in the margins of everything else — early mornings, the back booth at Remi's, the notes app on my phone at 2 a.m. when something comes back that I thought I'd forgotten.

What I have is the work, and I wanted you to know it exists.

If you've been reading Southern Legends, you've already been reading pieces of it. The people I've profiled are neighbors, in the old sense — people whose lives are threaded into the same ground mine is. I didn't plan it that way. That's just what happened when I started paying attention.

I'll keep writing the profiles. I'll keep building what I'm building. And I'll keep working on this book, which is the thing underneath everything else, the thing that makes sense of the rest of it.

The valley is still there. I'm still learning what it has to say.

Matt
