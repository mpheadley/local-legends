We had nine acres and a plan.

The plan was on graph paper, taped to the wall of a 1832 farmhouse. Beds squared off, rows numbered, a retail kiosk down by the trail. I am good at plans. I can look at a blank field and see the whole thing finished. That is a gift and it is also the thing that nearly killed me.

The farm is gone now. I will not walk you through how. But I learned something out there that I keep running into everywhere else, and I want to tell you about it, because it turns out it is the same lesson whether you are growing zinnias or growing a business or growing a person.

You can draw a blueprint for a building. You cannot draw one for a bed of flowers. The building obeys the plan. The flowers obey the soil, the rain, the thing that eats them at night, the frost that comes early the one year you needed it not to. You do not build a garden. You tend it. You show up and you pay attention and you stay with it until it turns.

Here is the part that took me longer to see. You cannot blueprint a soul either.

For the past year I have been teaching small business owners a thing I call the Plainspoken Blueprint. It is not complicated. Most businesses have their message backwards. They fix the website before the brand and the brand before the message, when the order runs the other way. Message first. Everything else after. I sit down with an owner for ninety minutes and we find the plain sentence underneath all the clever ones. Most people cry a little. I did not expect that when I started.

I thought I had built a marketing exercise. I had built something older than marketing.

Because the reason the owner cries is not the sentence. It is that for ninety minutes, in a room where nobody is grading them, they get to stop performing. They drop the corporate words. They stop trying to sound like a bigger company than they are. They say the true small thing they have been afraid was not enough. And it is enough. It was always enough.

A monk named Thomas Keating had a name for what happens in that room. He called it Divine Therapy. He was not talking about business. He was talking about prayer, about the way a person sits in silence long enough to let the false self relax its grip. Keating and the people around him, Merton and Finley and the rest, spent their lives on one idea. You are carrying around a self you built to be safe. It is not you. It is scaffolding. And underneath it, unhurt, is the person you actually are.

Merton had a word for the scaffolding. He said the false self covers itself in bandages to make itself feel real. Bandages. I had been using that exact word for two years, in a business framework, without knowing a Trappist got there first. The thing I ask an owner to name and set down, the pile of impressive language hiding the plain true thing, is the same pile a contemplative spends forty years learning to release.

So the eight moves I teach in a conference room map, one for one, onto eight things people like Keating and Richard Rohr have been saying about the soul. The customer becomes a mind quiet enough for compassion. The problem becomes learning to sit with the right question instead of grabbing the right answer. The bandage becomes the false self. The cost is the truth you only find at the bottom and the edges, never at the top. The guide is not a wise old teacher handing you a plan. The guide is a companion who climbs down into the wreckage with you.

I am not going to pretend I designed it that way. I designed a way to help a florist write a better homepage. But I have been a pastor for nineteen years and I have been in the wreckage myself, the real kind, the kind with a psych ward and a borrowed camper in my parents' backyard on the other side of it. And when I looked at the plain little framework I had made, I saw my own recovery in it. I saw the thing that saved me described in the language of a sales call.

You cannot blueprint a soul. But you can tend one. You can show up, drop the clever, say the true small thing, and stay with it until it turns.

That is the whole method. It works on a business. It worked on me. Keating would tell you it works on prayer, and that all three are the same work wearing different clothes.

I am writing a book about it. I do not know yet if it is one book or a chapter of another. But I know it is true, because I have watched it be true in a conference room and in a flower bed and on the worst night of my life, which is three places that should have nothing in common and turn out to have the only thing that matters.
