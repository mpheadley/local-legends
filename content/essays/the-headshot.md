The actual headshot is pretty good.

I'm standing in front of a bookshelf. Flannel shirt, blue-gray. Blue eyes. A smile that doesn't look like I'm about to litigate anything. My head is bald in the way bald heads are, which is to say completely: no comb-over, no regret. The whole composition has the energy of a man who just read something encouraging and hasn't decided what to do about it yet.

I've used it on every platform I own. Three websites. A LinkedIn. The footer of every email I send under the name Gather Studio, which is the company I run, which is mostly me.

Last April, I asked an AI to draw it.

The result arrived in under thirty seconds. It is (and I want to be careful here) a portrait of someone. There is a head. Two eyes. A mouth. These details are technically accurate. What the AI produced is a bald man who appears to be smiling through significant distress, rendered in the style of MS Paint by someone who had the concept of a face described to them over the phone. The eyes are brown. (Mine are blue.) They are also slightly different sizes and pointed in slightly different directions, the way eyes look when they are discovering that something has gone wrong but have not yet agreed on what. The mouth is the size of a small country. The ear (there is only one visible) could receive satellite signals.

It is currently saved in a folder on my hard drive labeled `headshots-stylized`, next to the hedcut illustration, the Ghibli watercolor, and the terracotta clay bust that also doesn't look like me but at least looks like a person someone would trust.

I have told no one about it until now.

---

Here is what I have not told you yet: the AI that drew that portrait is not some tool I found on the internet. I built this. All of it. I have a software company that runs on AI. I have an AI assistant named Iris who manages my calendar, drafts my emails, monitors my business pipeline, and posts to social media on my behalf. I have a 3,000-word document I wrote for an AI that describes how I think, what I find funny, who my family is, and what words I'm never allowed to use in any output attributed to me.

The document exists so that AI can write in my voice.

The AI cannot draw my face.

These two facts are sitting in the same folder on my hard drive and I am trying to decide what to do with them.

---

My operating principle (I wrote this down, I mean I actually wrote it down) is that disclosed AI runs the operations of every venture I own, as fully and as fast as it safely can, 24/7, in public. I announced this to my newsletter. I put it on my website. I believe it. The AI that runs my business is always labeled: *this is Iris, Matt's AI assistant.* No one is deceived. That's the whole point.

What I did not anticipate is that full disclosure includes the portrait.

Full disclosure is: the machine that writes my bio cannot draw my eyes. The machine that schedules my calls does not know what color they are. I have given it everything: the sermons, the manic episode, the camper in my parents' backyard, the letter to my kids I haven't finished, the thing Heather said that I'm still carrying. And it can tell the story back to me with uncanny accuracy, and it cannot find blue in the eyes it constructs.

There is something clarifying about that.

---

I spent seven years as a pastor. Before that, a worship leader. Before that, a music student at Jacksonville State, singing Sweeney Todd in the auditorium on a Tuesday night in front of an audience that included my mother and a woman I was trying to impress. The credential is long and varied and mostly involves standing in front of people and asking them to trust you with something important.

What I have learned, across all of it, is the difference between being known and being rendered.

Rendered is easy. You give someone the facts: the bio, the headshot, the list of credentials. And they assemble a version of you that functions in their mind as an adequate substitute for the real thing. This is fine. This is most of what happens between people. It is all that can happen between a person and a machine.

Known is different. Known is slower. Known is the person who calls when they shouldn't have to, who remembers the thing you said two years ago that you were hoping everyone had forgotten, who can walk into the room and tell from eight feet away that you are not okay. Known is not assembled from facts. It builds up from sitting with someone long enough that you stop presenting and they stop evaluating and something true happens in the space between you.

No AI has ever known me.

But one of them tried to draw my face, and I find I can't stop looking at it.

---

The portrait is still there. Folder: `headshots-stylized`. File name, which I will not reproduce here because it is longer than some essays I have written, begins with the date and the word *mspaint* and ends with a description of a bald man with an enormous smile that was accurate in the way all wrong descriptions are accurate: it got the outline right and missed the actual thing entirely.

I know what it is. It is a machine doing its best with what it had. It had pixels and prompts and probability and no idea what blue looks like when it lives in a face that has been crying.

That's not a criticism. That's just what rendered looks like.
