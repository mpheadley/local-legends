I knew who he was but didn't feel I could take up space or tell him how I knew of him.

That was 2023, in the hallway at Anniston First. Shannon Jenkins was meeting with Kyle and Tammy. I stopped when I saw them. Shannon looked up and shook my hand — eye contact, full attention, the real thing. I didn't know it had stuck.

I'd heard his name for years before that. A friend at Weaver First had told me about him back around 2019, when I was dealing with homelessness issues in Weaver as the pastor there. She said Shannon was doing good work helping Anniston reduce the duplication of resources and that I should connect with him. I knew his reputation long before I knew his face.

But I didn't feel I could take up space. So I filed it away.

A few months later we bumped into each other at a gas station in Golden Springs. Brief exchange. Both moving on. A few days after that, he sent me a Facebook message.

*"I had been dealing with some not so pleasant things that morning,"* he wrote, *"and just seeing your familiar face helped."*

---

Shannon grew up in rural White Plains, Alabama, where his family grew their own food. He didn't know that made them poor. That realization came later — not from the hardship itself but from finally seeing what had been just past the edge of his horizon. *"I felt ashamed, really,"* he said.

---

He went to Jacksonville State for marketing but thought he was headed for full-time career ministry. Did music ministry at his church. Had a difficult experience there and realized it wasn't for him. Worked at Movie Gallery as a college student, renting out VHS tapes — went the way of the dinosaurs, he said, and we laughed. Then took over a graphic design business from his wife — she had started it before print shops had a graphic designer on staff. His computer was an early Apple, first model, something like eight megabytes of memory. He laughed. He was working from home and did not enjoy being home alone.

He knew the director of United Way at the time — a member of his church — and asked for an opportunity to get out of the house. The director hired him for two falls, about twenty-five hours a week for three months, helping with the annual fundraising campaign.

That's when his eyes opened. He had missed it — probably grew up in poverty but didn't know it, insulated in White Plains. United Way made it visible.

He never left.

---

Twenty-two years later, he runs an organization with thirty-two staff, nine social workers, and programs of its own. When he started, there were none. *"What a relief that could be,"* he told me.

United Way purchased the building from the church — a two-story complex the congregation called the Bridge, gym and kitchen and meeting rooms and a literal bridge connecting it to the education wing. Same campus where Shannon and I first crossed paths, back when he was in conversations with the staff about hosting a warming station.

---

In June 2024, I was going through my first depressive episode. Shannon was one of the few people that kinda kept up with me — checking in, encouraging me to seek psychiatric help, telling me it was going to be okay.

He wrote to me: *"Honoring my time doesn't mean you have to act or be any certain way. I am happy to just provide the company — no expectations of any deep conversations. Time with a friend is never time wasted."*

Some time before that — before my leave of absence — I ran into him at Full Belly Deli, right next door to the Freedom Riders National Monument. He stopped. Turned. Looked dead at me and took his time: *"Don't be afraid to dream."*

Later, after I had resigned and was throwing ideas at him — nonprofit concepts, church plants, a flood of them — he listened and then gently said I should pace myself, because people could get overwhelmed if I told them about everything all at once.

---

He now trains his United Way staff on Donald Miller's StoryBrand framework — the same tool I use in my web design work. Shannon had a marketing background before he ever got to United Way.

He saw it at Family Christian Bookstore — *Blue Like Jazz* — with a warning label on the cover. He picked it up immediately.

*"It was a book about deconstruction,"* he said, *"before deconstruction even had a name. It totally changed my life."*

I think about his message — *"just seeing your familiar face helped"* — sent to a man who didn't feel he could take up space.
