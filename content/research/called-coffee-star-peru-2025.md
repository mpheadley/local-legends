# Anniston Star: "From Peru to pour-overs" — June 19, 2025
By Timothy Cash, Managing Editor

## Full text

ANNISTON — Jerod Snider never imagined he'd be running a coffee shop, much less one that started with a bag of beans and a mission trip to the Andes Mountains.

"I really don't like talking about how we got into coffee," Snider told members of the Calhoun County Rotary Club with a laugh. "So I'm going to take you through our journey of how we wound up there. It's never something we imagined we'd do, and never anything we planned to do."

In 2016, Snider and his wife, Hannah, sold everything and moved to Huancayo, Peru, with their 8-month-old daughter. The couple had long dreamed of nonprofit work and helping plant a church in the mountainous region. Over the next year, they distributed food, mentored youth and worked to turn over the church's operations to local leadership.

Then came a gift that changed everything: a small Ziploc bag of coffee. One of the local Peruvians, Elisa, brought him some coffee from her dad's farm.

"I had no idea they had a coffee farm," he said. "I tasted it, and at that time, it was the best coffee I had ever had."

That bag of beans launched what became Called Coffee. Snider agreed to help Elisa's father, Gregorio, try to bring the beans to the U.S. He didn't know how, but he said yes anyway.

He traveled 12 hours into the jungle to visit the farm, hauling water filters donated by a North Carolina nonprofit, Wine to Water. It was the first of many visits to La Vida Dor, Gregorio's hillside coffee operation. Over time, Snider learned the intricacies of specialty-grade coffee — how it's picked, de-pulped, washed, dried, graded and roasted.

"We just agreed to help them try to get it to the U.S.," he said. "And I had no idea how to do that."

After connecting with Non-Fiction Coffee Co. in Birmingham, which agreed to buy as much as he could send, Snider paid a young exporter $6,000, trusting the beans would arrive. They did.

Soon after, the Sniders moved back to the U.S. for the birth of their second child — an event that brought serious complications that may not have been survivable in Peru.

In late 2018, Snider launched Called Coffee with a pair of handmade carts and an espresso machine from Facebook Marketplace. Events like the Noble Street Festival marked their humble beginnings.

"That was a whole lot of work," Snider said. "I was like, 'We need a coffee truck.'"

The truck debuted just in time for the 2019 Festival of Lights in Oxford, where Called Coffee faced 300 customers on its first night.

"We had never done it before. We had no idea what we were doing, and we just started making drinks. And we survived that night and ran out of everything," he said.

That trial by fire gave Snider and his team real-time training and the income to support Gregorio's farm, where they helped build a proper wash tank, drying beds and even a road. They also saw the price Gregorio could earn for his beans increase from 70 cents to nearly $8 per pound.

"Where originally he was selling his coffee for five soles a kilo — this last year, it's gotten so good, we were able to pay 28 soles per kilo," Snider said. "I'm super proud of that."

The farm's success even brought new energy to Gregorio's family. His son, Eddie, who once wanted nothing to do with the coffee business, now runs a shop in Huancayo featuring his father's beans.

In 2020, the pandemic halted Snider's coffee event calendar, but with a building already under contract, he shifted his focus to creating a brick-and-mortar shop in Anniston.

Located in the former Daylight Donuts building, Called Coffee opened in February 2023 with a drive-thru that now accounts for 70 percent of its business.

"We've been so blessed with just an amazing team," Snider said. "Our goal is, when you come in, you just feel welcome."

The shop's name reflects its mission. "Called Coffee is — we believe that everybody's called to do something," Snider said. "There's always something you can do to help somebody else out."

That mission extends to local outreach. Called Coffee faced the delicate task of welcoming everyone — including those experiencing homelessness — while also creating a safe, comfortable environment for customers. The opening of Martha's Hope — a local shelter — helped meet those needs in a more sustainable way.

"We went from having daily problems … to I think we've only had, honestly, one or two incidents since Martha's Hope," Snider said. "It's been a huge sigh of — really — I'm glad I just get to support it."

As for what's next, Snider isn't sure.

"I've never started it with an intention of doing any of this. I was just trying to sell a bag," he said. "Still don't know what's next."
