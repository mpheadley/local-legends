# CLT Bicentennial 2032 — Seed Idea

## What It Is
A multi-year media and community campaign building toward the 200-year anniversary of the Treaty of Cusseta (1832) — the year Chief Ladiga signed away the Creek land that became the railroad that became the Chief Ladiga Trail.

Target year: **2032**

## Origin
Conceived during Matt's manic episode (spring 2025) while walking the trail. Died when the farm sold. Resurrected on a voice walk April 24, 2026 — Matt realized the idea isn't dead, just early.

The farmhouse on Matt's farm was built in 1832 — the same year Ladiga signed the treaty. Matt had a copy of the deed. That coincidence is the emotional entry point.

## The Idea
Write the CLT profile on Southern Legends first. Share it with Toby Bennington. Use it as a launchpad to ask Toby for an intro to Muscogee Creek Nation contacts. Let them lead on what the campaign looks like.

**Potential partners:**
- City of Anniston
- Muscogee (Creek) Nation cultural office (Oklahoma — descendants of the removed)
- Poarch Band of Creek Indians (Atmore, AL — only federally recognized tribe in Alabama)
- Rails to Trails Conservancy
- JSU Center for Economic Development
- Southern Legends (editorial/storytelling arm)

## Matt's Instinct
"I think I would take their lead on it." — voice walk, Apr 24 2026

Not Matt's campaign to run. Matt's role: write the story, make the introduction, hold the editorial thread.

## The Tension at the Center
A trail named after a man whose people were removed to build the railroad that became the trail. That's not irony — it's Alabama. The bicentennial is a chance to tell that story at scale, with the people whose story it actually is.

## What the Campaign Actually Is

A public reckoning, not a celebration.

Most bicentennials get planned as celebrations — ribbon cuttings, markers, official programming. This one has a structural problem: you can't celebrate 200 years of a trail named after a man whose removal built it without first naming what happened. The Muscogee Creek Nation gets to decide if and how they want to mark it. Matt's role — and Southern Legends' role — is to build the editorial groundwork that makes a real reckoning possible instead of another symbolic gesture.

That's the distinction that gives this campaign integrity and separates it from every other local history project: it starts with the people whose story was taken, and asks them what they want done with it.

## The Risk to Protect Against

Matt's instinct — "I think I would take their lead on it" — is the most important line in this document. The risk in a six-year arc is becoming the de facto lead organizer because he's the one with time, energy, and a website. That's how well-meaning white-led "history campaigns" happen and why they feel hollow.

Name that risk explicitly with Toby:

> "I want to support this, not run it. Who do I need to meet for that to be possible?"

That sentence does more work than any campaign plan.

## The 5-Year Arc

### Year 1 (2026–2027) — The editorial foundation

The CLT profile launches everything. Not because it announces the campaign — it doesn't, yet — but because it gives Matt a piece of real work to hand Toby. A good profile travels. Toby shares it. A Muscogee Creek cultural contact reads it. That's when the intro happens.

The profile does three jobs:
1. Tells the place story (trail, history, removal, 1832) through Matt's personal history with it
2. Establishes Matt as someone who gets the weight of it — the farmhouse deed, the shirt appropriation moment, the manic episode on the trail
3. Creates a reason to be in a room with Toby and eventually with the Creek Nation

After publishing: text Toby. Not email — use his number. Three sentences: "I published the CLT piece. I'd love for you to read it. And when you have time, I want to ask you something in person." When you meet: ask the 2032 question directly — not as a pitch, but as a genuine ask. "You've spent 15 years on this trail. Does the 200th anniversary of the Treaty of Cusseta mean anything to you? Is there something that should happen?" Let him name where the doors are.

### Year 2 (2027–2028) — The question

If the Creek Nation is willing to engage: a joint conversation about what *they* want from 2032. Not what Anniston wants. Not what Rails to Trails wants. Possible answers:
- A dedicated website
- A traveling exhibition
- A physical marker or trail monument
- A trail renaming conversation
- A private ceremony with no public campaign at all

Any of those is valid. Matt's job at this stage is listening and documenting.

If the Creek Nation declines or is unreachable through Toby's contacts: the campaign becomes something smaller but still real — a long-form SL documentary piece, a formal marker proposal through the City of Anniston, a written record that enters the historical archive. Still worth doing. Just honest about its limits.

### Year 3–4 (2028–2030) — The infrastructure

If partnerships are forming: a dedicated site. Not to lead — to house. A URL that can be shared by the City, by Rails to Trails, by the Creek Nation's cultural office. A place for oral histories, the timeline, the maps. A site that outlasts the 2032 event.

Also in this window: the artist commission. A Creek artist creates the portrait that goes on the trail, on the site, maybe eventually on a marker. This is a two-year lead-time project — don't wait until 2031.

### Year 5–6 (2030–2032) — The event

Whatever the Creek Nation wants it to be. Matt shows up as the editorial record-keeper, not the organizer. The SL CLT piece gets updated with new material. The dedicated site goes live. A ceremony happens — led by the people whose story it is.

## Campaign Ideas

### Artistic / Physical
- **Monument or marker on the trail** — not a standard bronze plaque. Commissioned by a Creek artist, placed at a meaningful point on the corridor. Permanent, physical, hard to ignore.
- **Artist-in-residence on the trail** — a Creek artist spends time on the corridor and makes work responding to it. Portrait, site-specific installation, or something temporary. The process is the story as much as the product.
- **Land acknowledgment at the trailheads** — real text written by the Creek Nation cultural office, installed at every access point. Simple, permanent, daily reach. No infrastructure required.

### Archival
- **Oral history archive** — audio recordings with Muscogee Creek descendants about what this land meant. Hosted on the dedicated site, submitted to the Alabama Department of Archives. Outlasts the campaign.

### Event
- **A single ceremony in 2032** — led entirely by the Creek Nation, whatever form they choose. Southern Legends documents it. No stage, no ribbon, no mayor's speech unless they ask for one.

### Long-Shelf-Life
- **A book** — the CLT profile as the seed chapter. The campaign becomes the reporting. By 2030 there's enough for something publishable: part local history, part memoir, part reckoning. The 1832 farmhouse deed is the opening. This may be the strongest commercial book idea in the archive — it has national audience (Indigenous land rights, trail culture, Southern history), a built-in narrative arc with a real deadline, and Matt is the narrator with proximity rather than the hero. The structure is already there. If the Creek Nation engages and relationships are documented by 2028, there's a real proposal. If they decline, it becomes a long-form magazine piece instead.
