## What is NEH?

**National Endowment for the Humanities** — a federal agency that funds humanities projects across America. Public history, oral history, museum exhibits, documentary journalism, scholarly books. One of the most important funders in this space. Requires institutional affiliation or fiscal sponsorship for most programs — but has one individual-facing track (Public Scholars) that's relevant here.

## Am I Credentialed Enough to Be a Paid Public Historian?

**Short answer: not yet, but this project builds you toward it.**

"Public historian" is not a licensed profession — it's a role you grow into through demonstrated work. You don't need a history PhD. What funders and institutions actually look at:

1. **Published work** — articles, profiles, essays in legitimate outlets
2. **Rigorous sourcing** — primary sources, oral history, documented research
3. **Community partnership** — especially with the affected community (Creek Nation)
4. **Institutional backing** — a museum, university, or nonprofit co-signing your work

Matt has the storytelling skills, 19 years of pastoral credibility in NE Alabama, the local relationships, and the site-specific personal history. What he's building toward: a published book and a documented Creek Nation relationship. Those two things, combined, make "paid public historian" legitimate in any grant application.

The CLT profile is the first credential. The book is the second. The Creek Nation relationship is the third. You don't need all three to start — you need the first to get the next.

## NPS Trail of Tears National Historic Trail — Research Findings (Apr 2026)

### What It Is
The Trail of Tears NHT commemorates forced removal of Native peoples in the late 1830s. ~2,200 miles across 9 states (AL, AR, GA, IL, KY, MO, NC, OK, TN). Official designation centers on **Cherokee removal (1838–1839)**, but NPS formally acknowledges five tribes — Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, **Muscogee Creek**, and Seminole — as part of the broader removal legacy. Creek removal happened 1834–1837, two years before Cherokee.

### The CLT Corridor and Trail of Tears
No documented connection between the Chief Ladiga Trail and official Trail of Tears routes. However: the corridor is in Creek removal territory, named for a Creek chief who signed the 1832 treaty that preceded removal. The connection is historically real — it's just not yet officially documented. **That gap is an opportunity.** Establishing the CLT as a Trail of Tears affiliated site would be an act of public history, not just paperwork.

Research needed: What were the actual Creek removal routes through Calhoun and Cleburne counties? Did any Creek removal parties travel through or near the CLT corridor? Dr. Tamara Levi and the Muscogee (Creek) Nation's Historic & Cultural Preservation Dept are the right sources for this.

### Affiliated Site Designation — How It Works
Voluntary partnership agreement with NPS. No property acquisition. Requirements:
- Historical significance related to Trail of Tears removal
- At least occasional public access
- Written agreement with NPS National Trails Office

**Benefits:**
- Display official Trail of Tears logo
- Eligibility for NPS funding and technical assistance (resource management, interpretation, signage, education)
- Access to National Park Foundation Active Trails grants ($3.8M+ distributed since 2008)
- Public recognition as official NPS trail partner

**Contact:** National Trails Office, 1100 Old Santa Fe Trail, Santa Fe NM 87505 — ntir_information@nps.gov — (505) 988-6098

### Existing Alabama and Georgia Certified Sites
**Alabama (4 sites):** All in Lauderdale/Colbert counties (Tuscumbia Landing, Waterloo Landing) — northwest Alabama, not NE Alabama. No certified sites in Calhoun or Cleburne counties. **Gap.**

**Georgia (12 sites):** Chief Vann House, New Echota, Chieftains Museum/Major Ridge Home, others — all in northwest Georgia near the Cherokee corridor. None in the Silver Comet corridor counties (Paulding, Polk, Haralson).

**The implication:** If the CLT corridor can be documented as a Creek removal route, it would be the first Trail of Tears certified site in NE Alabama and one of the few Creek-specific (vs. Cherokee-specific) sites in the entire system. That's nationally significant.

### How to Pursue
1. Research whether Creek removal parties traveled through the CLT corridor — ask Tamara Levi and the Muscogee Nation's Historic & Cultural Preservation Dept
2. If documented: contact NPS National Trails Office, request evaluation for affiliated site certification
3. With certification: apply for NPF Active Trails grants, request NPS interpretive signage on the trail
4. This also strengthens every other grant application — "NPS-recognized Trail of Tears affiliated site" on a NEH or AHA application changes the conversation

### Trail of Tears Association (ToTA)
Nonprofit with NPS cooperative agreement. Contact them alongside NPS — they have a membership structure, state chapters, and can advocate for Creek-specific recognition within the Trail of Tears framework. nationaltota.com

## SCT Land History — Key Research Finding (Apr 2026)

The Silver Comet Trail corridor (Paulding, Polk, Haralson counties, NW Georgia) was NOT covered by the Treaty of Cusseta (1832). Different treaties, same Creek Nation, same removal era:

- **Treaty of Indian Springs (1825)** — Lower Creek chief William McIntosh signed without Creek Council authorization. The Creek Nation **executed McIntosh** for it (May 31, 1825).
- **Treaty of Washington (1826)** — Opothleyahola (Upper Creek orator) challenged McIntosh's treaty and negotiated a corrected version. Ceded all Creek lands in Georgia.
- **Treaty at Creek Agency (1827)** — Fixed a surveying mistake; Paulding and Haralson counties were accidentally left out of the 1826 treaty. This corrected it.
- **Treaty of Cusseta (1832)** — Alabama only. Chief Ladiga. Five years after the Georgia cessions.

**Why this matters for the book:** The corridor isn't just two trails — it's the physical trace of a decade-long political unraveling of Creek sovereignty. Two states, multiple chiefs, an execution, a resistance, and then removal. McIntosh's story (executed by his own nation for unauthorized land sale) is one of the most dramatic chapters in Creek history. Opothleyahola is a major figure. The whole arc ends with Ladiga signing the Alabama cession in 1832.

This makes the corridor story **much richer** than a single treaty. It also means the book has two states, multiple protagonists, and a political drama spanning 1825–1832.

**No book exists on any of this.** The field is entirely open.
