I used to call it Wadiga. I didn't know how else to say it.

I've been walking and running the Chief Ladiga Trail since college — since I had an apartment in Jacksonville where the trail crossed Highway 204 and I'd head toward Piedmont and back just to have somewhere to go. I pastored a church in Weaver for six years with the trailhead a quarter mile from the parsonage. I taught all four of my kids to ride bikes on it. I ran with the twins in a double stroller at the Woodstock 5K, their little cheeks bouncing, strangers smiling as we passed.

I've cried on it. I've brainstormed on it. During the worst year of my life I walked it for hours at a time, talking into my phone, trying to think my way out of something you can't think your way out of.

I never really thought about who it was named for.

Chief Ladiga was a leader of the Muscogee Creek Nation. In 1832 he signed the Treaty of Cusseta, ceding the land that would become a railroad corridor — the same corridor that was converted to this trail. His people were removed. The railroad was built. The railroad was abandoned. And now there's a trail named after him, and most people who walk it every day don't know any of this.

The farmhouse I lived in during those years was built in 1832. The same year. I had a copy of the deed.

I've been carrying an idea for a while — a public history campaign building toward the 200th anniversary of the treaty in 2032. A chance to tell that story at scale, with the people whose story it actually is.

It's early. The partnerships aren't formed yet. But the project has a home: **[chiefladiga.org](https://chiefladiga.org)**.

A full profile is coming. This is just the seed.
