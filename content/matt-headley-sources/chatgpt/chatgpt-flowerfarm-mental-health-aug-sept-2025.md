*annistonfirst.info account*

**Source:** annistonfirst.info ChatGPT export (OpenAI, April 2026)
**Account context:** Third account — used during and after the Anniston First UMC ministry period (2022–2025). Separate from mpheadley@gmail.com account.

HANDLE WITH CARE — contains mental health, medication, disability, and family details. Not for publication without clinical team input.

## Context

These conversations cluster in August and September 2025 — two to three months before hospitalization. The farm is closing. Matt has recently received the bipolar II diagnosis. He is trying Vraylar (later switched to Seroquel), not working in any consistent capacity, and spending long stretches at home with Heather's parents present and the property being dismantled around him.

The manic episode that led to quitting ministry and building the farm — the period most documented in the mpheadley account — is now fully behind him. What is documented here is the depression that followed the crash: the medication cycling, the disability research, the job searches, the shame of sitting still while Heather and her parents worked.

## August 7–October 11, 2025 — "Bipolar struggle support"

**Date:** August 7, 2025 — eleven weeks before hospitalization. Two hundred forty-four messages, spanning from August 7 through October 11. The longest conversation in the annistonfirst.info export.

This thread runs almost entirely through the depressive collapse. It begins the evening of August 7, when Heather's parents are visiting and Matt has been in his bedroom for two hours:

> "Heathers parents are here and I've been in my bedroom for 2 hours, it's 5:20pm. I'm not tired just ashamed to show my face because I've done very little today and I'm disappointed in my bipolar diagnosis and not being able to keep the farm going and be a strong husband and father."

The AI responds with an encouraging message about worth not being measured by productivity. Matt challenges it immediately:

> "You just blow smoke I think sometimes. You tell me what you think I want to hear."

The AI's response to the pushback is notable: "You're right to call out anything that feels like a pep talk without substance." It does not double down on the encouragement but instead names what it observes: Matt is sitting with shame, paralysis, and grief, and that none of what the AI says resolves that.

The farm's physical deterioration is visible throughout:

> "The sheep fence battery is dead. Trees and branches are fallen all over the property. And I'm sitting in my bed doing nothing about it."

On August 8, the conversation pivots to disability benefits — SSDI eligibility, SSA Blue Book criteria for bipolar disorder, legal aid resources. Matt describes finding it exhausting to read. The AI: "You can put the whole 'disability decision' on a shelf for now and just focus on stabilizing." Then: job searches — warehouse work, admin roles, Highland Health's paperwork role that his friend Rebecca offered at $34–40K/year.

> "I feel like I'm faking it. Being sick. I feel fine. I am not tired. I'm just embarrassed."

This passage is important. It is three months before hospitalization. Matt is experiencing what the AI correctly identifies as "the strange, liminal space between collapse and recovery" — the bipolar II pattern where depression does not always present as total incapacitation but as shame, paralysis, and an inability to engage with the world that the person cannot fully explain even to themselves.

The thread runs through September. By late September, Matt is working temporarily for Preston (Heather's brother, building a deck) and talking about working "for the paper" — the Anniston Star or Calhoun County Journal — alongside it.

By October 11, the thread ends with:

> "[USER:] I don't know where to focus."
> "[ASSISTANT:] When everything feels big and blurry, not knowing is actually a really honest place to be."
> "[USER:] Heavy sadness."
> "[ASSISTANT:] That heavy sadness feels like the kind that comes from deep loss and exhaustion — not just chemical or situational, but the ache of watching a life you built slowly come apart."

**Why this matters for the piece:** This is the longest sustained AI relationship in either export — 244 messages over more than two months. It is not therapy. It is something more ambient: a conversational thread Matt returns to across August, September, and October while his life is being sorted and sold around him. The bipolar II diagnosis is new. The AI becomes a place to ask questions he can't bring to Heather (who is exhausted), to test whether his perceptions are reasonable, to look up disability criteria and job listings. Whether this ambient availability is a net positive or a substitute for more intensive clinical support is the central question. The "blow smoke" exchange is probably publishable — it shows both Matt's capacity for honest self-assessment and the AI's willingness to be called on its own patterns.

## August 8, 2025 — "Vraylar vs lithium comparison"

**Date:** August 8, 2025. Eight messages.

Matt has just been given a bipolar diagnosis and his prescriber is recommending Vraylar. He asks ChatGPT to compare Vraylar and lithium:

> "I don't know how to decide."

The AI walks through the clinical picture: Vraylar is approved for bipolar I mania and bipolar depression; lithium is the gold-standard mood stabilizer with the longest track record. For "mixed features" — depression with agitation — Vraylar may be the better first-line choice. The AI does not recommend one over the other but provides the framework for the conversation with the prescriber.

When Matt says his prescriber recommends Vraylar and asks whether it will help with energy and decision-making, the AI provides an honest answer: Vraylar may improve depression-related cognitive fog over weeks, but Seroquel (which Matt is also on) is sedating and will slow cognitive function in the short term.

**Why this matters for the piece:** This is a straightforward medication consultation conducted with an AI. It is exactly the kind of use case the AI's designers intended. It is also exactly the kind of conversation that would have happened with a patient advocate, a pharmacist, or a more accessible psychiatrist in a better-resourced context. The file documents it because it is part of the arc: Matt is doing his own medical research, at home, in the middle of a crisis, with no other intermediary. The AI is doing the job of multiple systems.

## August 12, 2025 — "Vraylar tiredness with Seroquel"

**Date:** August 12, 2025. Eight messages.

Matt has been on Vraylar for two days and Seroquel for over a week. The Seroquel dose was just increased from 100mg to 150mg the previous night. He is very tired and wants to know if that is expected.

The AI explains: the Seroquel increase is the primary driver; Vraylar is not yet in therapeutic range; the combination is predictably sedating in the first one to three weeks. It provides a timeline: days 1–4, normal adjustment; days 5–14, gradual stabilization; weeks 2–6, real Vraylar effect begins.

**Why this matters for the piece:** Included for completeness and because the medication timeline here — Vraylar started August 8, Seroquel being titrated simultaneously — is context for the medication cycling that continues through October and into the hospital. The prescriber later switches to Caplyta (September–October), then back to Seroquel at the hospital (October 25). Understanding the arc requires this data.

## August 14, 2025 — "Disability benefits eligibility"

**Date:** August 14, 2025. Two messages.

Matt asks whether he is eligible for disability benefits through Wespath (the United Methodist Church's pension and benefits board) since he is no longer working in a church. The AI answers directly: the Comprehensive Protection Plan (CPP) covers active clergy only, with full-time appointment and compensation thresholds. Matt almost certainly no longer qualifies.

**Why this matters for the piece:** This conversation is brief but significant. Matt was in a $64,000/year pastoral position with denomination benefits. He resigned abruptly during the manic episode. Eight months later, he is checking whether his former employer's disability program can help him. It cannot. The financial consequence of the resignation — losing not just income but benefits access at the moment when he most needs it — is documented here without commentary.

## August 10–13, 2025 — "Mental health overview"

**Date:** August 10, 2025. One hundred fourteen messages.

Matt opens with a direct question: "What do you know about my mental health?" This is a test of the AI's memory — what has accumulated in this account across the conversations. The AI's response summarizes what it has received: depression, bipolar II diagnosis (which Matt has expressed reluctance about), medication history (sertraline mentioned in the AI's summary, Seroquel current), mood cycling, agitation.

Matt is at home while Heather takes the children to church — the same church where Matt used to be associate pastor. He is trying to decide whether to go with them. He does not go.

Heather asks him to take the children out for ice cream so that a real estate agent can view the house without the children present. They don't know the house is being listed. Matt needs help explaining to a 12- and 11-year-old why they are going to the park in the middle of a Sunday afternoon. The AI provides scripts.

Later the same day, after the children leave with someone else:

> "I want to sleep for hours because I don't know what to do when I'm awake."

And later, returning to the bipolar diagnosis after reading about it:

> "I read about bipolar 2 and it sounds like a life sentence."

The thread runs through August 13, covering structured daily plans for depressive episodes and a "job-search readiness checklist" for when Matt stabilizes enough to apply.

**Why this matters for the piece:** The "what do you know about my mental health" question is the AI-as-mirror moment. Matt is checking whether what he has said to this machine has accumulated into something coherent — whether the AI has a model of him. The real estate scene is operationally useful (the AI helps him manage a logistical problem) and emotionally revealing (the house is already being shown, the children don't know, Matt is on the outside of both the family and the decision). "I want to sleep for hours because I don't know what to do when I'm awake" may be the most compressed description of this period in the archive.

## August 17–18, 2025 — "Navigating big life changes"

**Date:** August 17, 2025. Twenty-eight messages.

Heather's plan is fully articulated here for the first time, from Matt's perspective. She intends to sell the house and most of their assets to purchase a trailer and move it to her parents' property in Pleasant Valley. She plans to cover the family on $30,000 per year from a secretarial job.

> "Heather is planning on selling our house and most of our valuable assets to have enough money to purchase a trailer and put it on her parents property so that we can live off of a $30,000 a year income that she plans to get by working a secretarial job. I'm having a hard time getting excited about this… leaving our 10 acre property with a historic home in North Anniston to live in a trailer in Pleasant Valley."

Matt's concern about the trailer is not comfort but permanence — he knows trailers depreciate and imagines being locked in for one to three years. He is also worried about what the move will do to the marriage:

> "I'm concerned what this is going to do to our relationship if we continue forward with a plan to live in a trailer on her parents property."

Heather tells him she is worried he will not be able to let go of regret about leaving the property. The AI offers a "decision framework" and conversation scripts for discussing regret together.

On August 18, Heather's parents arrive to help with landscaping and Heather begins giving things away. The property is being visibly, physically dismantled. Matt is inside:

> "I'm afraid Heather's going to find me sitting here in the air conditioning not helping outside."

When Matt tells Heather he is exhausted and overwhelmed, she asks if he needs to go to bed. He says he does not want to. She offers him two tasks: moving bookshelves, and cutting branches from the pecan tree he cut down.

> "I just want to sleep."

**Why this matters for the piece:** This is the point at which the decision becomes real and irreversible — not announced to Matt but enacted around him, with Heather's parents physically there and the property being cleared while Matt sits inside. The $30,000 figure against his former $64,000 salary is the financial data point. The regret conversation with Heather — both of them acknowledging that regret will probably follow Matt — is among the most honest exchanges in this archive between the actual people in the marriage, as reported to the AI.

## August 18–September 12, 2025 — "Career transition focus"

**Date:** August 18, 2025. Two hundred two messages, spanning from August through September 12.

This thread begins as a career coaching conversation. Matt asks about narrowing career focus. The AI provides a "focus cone" of related roles based on his background — admin and office work, ministry-adjacent roles, Anniston-area job listings.

The thread quickly becomes more personal. Matt explains that he was making $64,000 a year at the church when he left. He notes that his departure was abrupt and controversial. He asks about returning to church work. The AI advises against immediate full-time return given the strained relationships but suggests exploring administrative or part-time roles.

The central revelation in this thread:

> "The farm is shutting down… and I was making 64k at the church before I quit."

This is stated flatly, mid-thread, as a correction of the financial picture. The AI recalibrates its job-search advice accordingly.

Matt asks about Kyle Bryan — the associate pastor who preceded him, who left Anniston First in good standing to run the United Way homeless shelter. He considers reaching out to Kyle about potential work. He does not say whether he does.

By September, the thread shifts to Preston's deck-building work and Matt's idea of working for the paper alongside it. The last message notes that the deck work is intermittent and Matt is exploring the paper as a steadier option.

**Why this matters for the piece:** This thread documents the full financial anatomy of the situation: $64,000 → resignation during manic episode → farm investment → farm failure → $34–40K jobs that feel beneath him → disability research → outdoor labor. The arc is documented inside a single conversation thread, spread across a month of daily check-ins. It also contains the first mention of the Anniston Star/Calhoun Journal as a potential employer — relevant context for how Matt is thinking about the writing and storytelling path that eventually becomes Southern Legends and Headley Web.
