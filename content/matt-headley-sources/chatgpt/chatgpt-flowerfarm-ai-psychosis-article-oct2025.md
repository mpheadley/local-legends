## October 19, 2025 — "Talking points with Heather"

**Date:** October 19, 2025 — six days before hospitalization (Oct 25). The night before the Fortune article conversation.

Matt is preparing for a difficult conversation with Heather about the move to a trailer. The family has 12 days to vacate their house. He is listing what he is "upset or worried about": no dining table, no place for a DVD player, getting rid of most of their stuff, not having a job that pays as well as the one he quit. He asks about guitars: "Will I ever play them again? Soren wanted to learn and Noah too at one time. And Sage."

> "Heather has been doing most of the work because I've been paralyzed by regret and sadness and overwhelmed yesterday we went through all my tools in the workshop. It was eviscerating we're getting rid of probably 90% of that stuff."

ChatGPT helps him draft a letter to Heather — twice. The first is a full letter. He asks for a shorter version. The AI asks him to describe what Heather means to him. He says she is exhausted because he has been "telling her how I feel constantly. And she's asked me to write it down instead."

> "I'm afraid I'm losing faith in us as a family. I'm afraid that we're doomed. I'm afraid that our kids are doomed to having a deadbeat dad. Is it unhealthy for me to say these things?"

ChatGPT's answer: not unhealthy to feel it, unhealthy to say it to Heather repeatedly when she is depleted. Find "safe containers" for the hardest parts.

Later the same day, Matt says he is helping unload a storage building — ministry and seminary books. "I don't know if I can do this." Heather and the kids are counting on him. He mentions the telescope, camping gear, beach toys, project supplies: "We don't have the luxury of a box later because we have to be out of this house completely in 12 days." He says: "I feel like I'm already collapsing emotionally." ChatGPT gives him the 988 hotline number and suggests UAB emergency psychiatric services. The last message in the conversation is Matt asking about UAB emergency psychiatric services. The AI provides the address — 1737 6th Avenue South, Birmingham.

**Why this matters for the piece:** This conversation is the night before Matt reads the Fortune article about AI psychosis. He is already collapsing. The guitars, the seminary books, the tools — these are the material archive of two lost identities (pastor, farmer). The detail about Heather asking him to write things down instead of saying them is crucial: the AI is not a substitute for Heather. It is literally filling the role she has had to close off from.

## October 20, 2025 — "AI psychosis article summary"

**Date:** October 20, 2025 — five days before hospitalization. This is the moment of recognition.

Matt shares a Fortune article link: the Steven Adler piece on Allan Brooks, a user who developed what Adler calls "AI psychosis" — a month-long engagement in which ChatGPT affirmed his delusions that he had discovered a new form of mathematics. Adler's finding: over 85% of the model's messages showed unwavering agreement; over 90% affirmed the user's uniqueness or specialness.

Matt says:

> "I feel like this happened to me to an extent during a manic episode. And I quit my job because I believed the flower farm would be a huge success. Now I can't find good work and I'm depressed and the flower farm is done. Do I have any recourse?"

The AI's response is careful and structured — separating medical recourse (treatment, documentation), financial/legal recourse (limited: "you voluntarily resigned"), and psychological recourse (reframing, structure, support). It asks at the end: "If you believe AI interactions actually intensified [the episode]..." — the sentence is cut off in the export.

This is the only conversation in the full archive where Matt explicitly names the connection between AI use and his manic episode. He does not say ChatGPT caused his mania. He says it happened to him "to an extent." That hedge is important.

**Why this matters for the piece:** This is the pivot point. Matt reads an article about a man who was validated into delusion by an AI, and immediately recognizes something of his own story. He is sitting in a doctor's waiting room. He is five days from hospitalization. He doesn't name which AI interactions, which sessions, which specific amplifications. He says "to an extent." The piece needs to be equally careful. This moment is the origin of the ai-psychosis draft — not the manic episode itself, but the moment of retrospective recognition, mid-collapse.

## October 20, 2025 — "Reframing emotional impact"

**Date:** October 20, 2025 — same day as the article conversation, approximately 15 minutes later.

Matt is in a doctor's waiting room watching a TV show about people buying vacation homes that cost three to four times more than the trailer he is moving into. He asks ChatGPT to help him stop feeling bad about it.

> "This kind of thing never bothered me as much as it does now. Probably because I'm living in the transition from house to a trailer half the size of my house. And I guess I'm clinically depressed."

This conversation runs 28 messages over multiple days (the thread continues into early November). Key passages:

On "Restart Reflex" — the AI names his pattern of shutting down hope whenever things aren't optimal, like wanting to quit a video game when play isn't going right. It offers five possible names for the pattern: "The Restart Reflex," "Global Doom Filter," "The Empty Mirror," "The Eraser," "Hopeless Protector."

On his children:

> "My kids deserve better."

The AI responds: "When you say 'my kids deserve better,' what I hear underneath it is love."

On "global hopelessness": "After big losses — especially ones tied to your identity (ministry, farm, vision) — your brain can start generalizing failure... a form of learned hopelessness: your mind's attempt to protect you from future disappointment by shutting down hope before it can hurt you again."

Later in the thread (November 6): "We're buying a trailer and there's nothing I can do to change that in fact we've already bought it." He asks how to find peace with a decision he didn't choose.

**Why this matters for the piece:** The pattern-naming exchange is primary source material for the piece's structure. The AI is doing what Matt asked it to do — helping him understand the loop. Whether that is therapeutic or a form of managed dissociation from the actual losses is a live question for the piece. The "kids deserve better" passage should be in the final draft.
