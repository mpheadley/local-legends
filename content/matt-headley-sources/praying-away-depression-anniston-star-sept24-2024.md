## Document (verbatim)

Maybe this is my Anniston Star Article?

I have been overwhelmed with the responses of love, affirmation and support that came from my post a couple of weeks ago where I 'outed myself' about my recent struggle with depression. I had no idea that so many people would respond so positively to what I said.

REWRITE OR OMIT Actually, I was prepared, and had been bracing myself for quite a while, for judgement or being discredited as unfit to lead, which for a time I was, particularly from my more conservative friends. It seems that my more conservative friends are just as ready to love and support me as my progressive ones.

One of the reasons i couldn't talk about depression until I had made signifigant progress in recovery was that my livelihood and financial security, my career, is dependent upon my ability to have a healthy body, mind, and spirit, like most people's careers are. But my vocation also comes with, almost invariably, with the unrealistic expectation that I must always be mentally well. I spent a lot of time considering alternative careers during my struggle with mental health. The scary part about depression is that it often comes with anhedonia, or the loss of interest in things that previously interested you or brought you satisfaction. I couldn't will myself to care about anything except my own depression - I had almost zero capacity for empathy or social interaction, even with my own children, let alone with friends or church members.

I expected to be shunned because I've heard about it and seen it firsthand, even recently. I was recently visiting a church member who had been hospitalized for weeks. This parishioner had been faithful participant in worship for decades, in both my church and several other churches in town, simultaneously. She also was connected to several other faith communities. She was no spring chicken and definitely would count as a seasoned person of faith. However, as I entered the room, she abruptly told me she was presently at God, that she didn't want to read Scripture. I told her I was sorry but that it was OK that she didn't want to read her Bible right now, and I could relate to feeling mad at God. (This RARELY happens when I visit people in the hospital - usually conversations tend toward affirming, building up, or even demonstrating one another's faith, at least on the surface...)

As we were sharing, a nurse, who had recently entered the room and was quietly preparing the patient's medicine, abruptly interjected and, after giving a disclaimer that they weren't a minister, insisted that it surely was the devil who was to blame for her not wanting to read her Bible, and that she should read it anyway because that's why her faith was weak. After quoting several memorized passages from the psalms and some other parts I don't remember (I stopped listening as I began to worry how this was being received by my host, who who politely listening. After several minutes passed, the entheusiastic delivery of a testimony of great faith concluded, and the nurse politely excused themself from the room.

While this person may have been correct (insert Bible verses here demonstraing their argument had Biblical basis), I found the spirit of their comments to be condescending and more about demonstrating their own certitude and 'rightness' than consoloing or comfortaing a woman who was suffering. I don't want to be too harsh - they provided compassionate medical care, and after the lecture, he excused himself so that we could continue our conversation, after making sure she understood how to take the medicine he was leaving for her.

As the well-meaning evangelical nurse walked out of the room, the hospitalized parishioner looked at me and smirked. Her wry smile indicated she understood how very relieved I was that the nurse was finally descended from their soapbox... or was it a bully-pulpit? I'm not sure there was a difference...

I felt this well-meaning nurse's lecture was more motivated by his own discomfort with overhearing an honest conversation bout doubt and spiritual depression than it was about consoling a patient who was suffering.

I think this might be a clue to the reason why I haven't heard much from 'haters.' When I shared with people during my depression about the fact that I was sturggling, I was sometimes told what I should be doing (praying more, taking better care of myself, etc.) Or the conversation was quickly changed to a less uncomfortable topic. I can appreciate how disorienting it could be to hear one of the most spiritually credentialed people you know talk about having difficulty knowing where God is in their depression, or even expressing anger with God. Only the people most close to me. dared to give me advice. The trouble was, I had already been doing all of those things: prayer, meditation, yoga, exercise, rest, spending time with loved ones, outdoors... etc. etc. Nothing was helping I had explored every option I could conceive of, except the one that I was avoiding with my whole heart - pharmaceutical medication.

That's a whole other story. My ego had to die first.

Since this is getting long, I wrap it up with a question:
How do you respond to doubts about God or faith (your own or others?)
Have you ever been told you should be able to 'pray away' your depression, or other mental health struggle?
What have you found to be most helpful in your experiences with mental health, or those of your loved ones?
