## What's Working

**Objects as character.** The $600 building. The flowers bought for four years. The three-hole outhouse. The bell that still works. You don't explain what these things mean — you put them in and trust the reader. That trust is visible in the prose. It's the right call, and it's consistent across the work.

**Negative space.** "The local police, who'd been warned hours earlier, were nowhere to be found." The strength isn't what happened — it's the absence. Same with the Lewis Downing profile: the logic of seed packets and upholstery fabric in a paint store works because you don't explain it. "It made no sense on paper. It also made them impossible to replace." You do this across multiple pieces without it feeling like a tic.

**Narrator presence without ego.** You have skin in these stories — the farm, the market, the church — but the profiles stay on the subjects. This is the hardest thing to pull off in this kind of work. You're doing it.

**Sentence rhythm.** The writing moves with restraint. Short declarative sentences that land, then longer ones that build. "He bought flowers for four years." That progression — fact, action, consequence — creates weight without commentary.

## Where It Flattens

**The Aquality Farms documentation problem.** You open with the farmers market scene — which has life ("People started lining up") — and then move into specs: 24,000 heads of lettuce, 6,000 square feet, six years of research. Those details are real but they don't land. You hit them before the reader cares enough to hold them. The scene earned trust; the specs spent it.

**Pulled quotes without integration.** Some profiles stack direct quotes and let them sit. "The overarching theme in what we do is centered around health..." followed by three lines of block quote. The voice becomes interview transcript rather than interpretation. Your best work uses what people say without always marking it as quote. The sister's line in the Renee piece — "God was like, you're shutting down, girl" — is the most alive sentence in the whole body of work. It's integrated. It earns its place.

## The Renee Rice Draft

The most literary piece in the collection. "She is not sentimental about any of this. She is practical, which is a different thing." That distinction carries real weight. The outhouse opener ("The outhouse has three holes") is audacious in the way it trusts the reader to receive information without context.

It could go fuller — more of Renee's voice, more dialogue. Right now Renee is mostly monologue. But the bones are right, and the rhythm has something the other profiles are still building toward.

---

## The Big Picture

You're writing regional history in real time and you're not sentimentalizing it. That's the thing worth protecting.

The thread about *people and places together* is stronger than the thread about places alone. Jay Jenkins works because it's framed through flowers, through what he did with his Saturdays. Lewis Downing works because you knew him first through transaction (seeds, a hoe), then through proximity. Shannon Jenkins works because you can see the actual friendship across the text.

Your ear for narrative distance is good. Stay there.

## One Craft Note Worth Keeping

The coaching transcript from April 2026 names it: "Don't soften it. Let readers hear exactly how it sounded." And: "The piece doesn't need to resolve anything — just hold that confusion and betrayal without tidying it up."

You know this intellectually. The writing shows you know it. The risk isn't ignorance — it's the pull toward resolution when the material gets close.
