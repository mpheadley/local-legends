Flannery O'Connor did not think she was writing tragedy. She thought she was writing comedy.

This is the thing people miss. The grandmother in "A Good Man Is Hard to Find", the family slaughtered by the Misfit on a Georgia roadside. O'Connor considered that a story with a hopeful ending. The grandmother reaches out and touches the Misfit's face in the moment before he shoots her, calls him one of her own children. Grace arrives like a car crash. O'Connor said that for the hard of hearing you have to shout, and for the nearly blind you have to draw large and startling figures. She was not apologizing. She was describing her method.

Southern Gothic satire is the tradition that understands this. The wound is not the punchline. The gap between the wound and what we call it, that's the punchline.

I spent seven years as a pastor in Northeast Alabama. I have attended more church budget meetings than I care to count. I have sat in rooms where people argued for forty-five minutes about whether the lobby chairs should be beige or taupe, and then the same room voted down a line item for food bank contributions in ninety seconds flat. The beige-versus-taupe debate is not a Southern Gothic story. The juxtaposition is.

That gap, between what the institution claims to be and what it demonstrably does, is where Southern Gothic satire lives. It has lived there for a long time.

Randy Newman has been the most underrated Southern Gothic satirist alive for fifty years. "Rednecks," from 1974, is sung by a narrator who is both mocking the Southern white man and calling out Northern hypocrisy in the same breath, and the discomfort of not knowing who the joke is on is exactly the point. "Good Old Boys" is an entire album of the South laughing at itself while daring you to laugh with it. Most people don't know what to do with Randy Newman because he won't let you be comfortable about which side you're on.

Barry Hannah's short stories do something similar in prose. Violent, funny, grotesque, Southern. His characters are often men who have convinced themselves of something the reader can see is ridiculous, and the horror and the comedy arrive at the same moment.

*The Righteous Gemstones* is the most important contemporary Southern Gothic satire in any medium. Danny McBride's HBO series about a megachurch family dynasty, the wealth gospel, the private jets, the violence lurking under the praise music, gets it exactly right because it does not hate its characters. The Gemstones are monstrous and they are also genuinely trying, in their deeply broken way, to be faithful to something. That double vision, seeing the grotesque and the grace simultaneously, is what separates Southern Gothic satire from a simple takedown.

A simple takedown would be easy. The material writes itself. A televangelist with a private jet is not interesting. A televangelist with a private jet who is also, in some broken and real way, sincere, that is O'Connor territory.

In visual art, sculptor Herb Parker describes his work as Southern Gothic: occasionally grotesque, dark humor, absurdist social critique. Laura Tanner Graham in New Orleans makes drawings and installations that live in explicit conversation with O'Connor. The tradition is alive in painting and sculpture, mostly below the radar of the mainstream art world, which is itself a Southern Gothic situation.

In theater: *Sweeney Todd* is Gothic satire of Victorian capitalism. *Little Shop of Horrors* is Gothic satire of American ambition. The South has produced versions of this, community theater runs these shows in church fellowship halls while the church next door preaches exactly what the shows are satirizing, and nobody notices, and that is also a Southern Gothic situation.

The through-line in all of it is what O'Connor called the grotesque: the deformity that reveals rather than conceals, the violence that opens rather than closes, the joke that lands because something true is underneath it.

The South has always known how to do this. It does not always admit it.

The best satire about this place has always come from people who loved it and couldn't look away, who saw the gap between the sermon and the budget meeting, between the beautiful church on the hill and what was done in its name, between the story the South tells about itself and the story that actually happened.

That gap is inexhaustible.

It is also, if you have the right angle, very funny.
