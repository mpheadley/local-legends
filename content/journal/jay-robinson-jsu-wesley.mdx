We met at Chick-fil-A in Jacksonville. I was between things — the city job had not started yet, the farm was sold, and I was trying to figure out what came next. Jay Robinson was the kind of person who made it easy to be between things. He asked what I was building. I told him. He listened the way campus ministers learn to listen, with the part of the brain that is always asking what the real question is underneath the one you just said out loud.

Jay runs the Wesley Foundation at Jacksonville State University. He has been doing it long enough to have opinions about how campus ministry works and to say, plainly, that most of what passes for success in that world is measuring the wrong thing.

---

The thesis he kept coming back to was this: events-based ministry is dead.

Not struggling. Not in decline. Dead. The consumer model that measures success by attendance — how many students showed up, how many cards were signed, how many heads were counted — is measuring something that does not actually tell you whether the ministry is working. Jay's metric is different.

Friendships forged, not found.

The distinction matters. You can walk into a room and find people. Finding does not require anything from you. Forging is different. It requires heat, pressure, time, and the willingness to stay in contact long enough for something to change shape. A campfire is not an event. It is an environment where forging happens. You cannot manufacture a campfire the way you manufacture a worship night.

He was not being nostalgic. He was being structural. The question is not what kind of events to throw at students. The question is what kind of environment produces genuine friendship, and then how do you build that instead of the other thing.

---

He told me about a grant.

This is the part I keep thinking about. The Wesley Foundation had applied for and received a grant from an organization whose name I will not use here. It was a significant amount of money. It changed the ministry's capacity overnight. Programs that were not possible before became possible. Headcount went up. Things looked successful by the measures that grants reward.

Then the grant was cut.

The ministry had built its budget around the grant. When it disappeared, the budget had a hole in it that the ministry's actual revenue could not fill. They had become dependent on a subsidy that was never guaranteed, and the subsidy had made the dependency invisible until it was not.

Build something that does not require a subsidy to function. This sounds obvious. It is not obvious when the subsidy is sitting right in front of you and the programs it funds look good on paper.

---

He also said something about artificial intelligence that I have been turning over since May.

The average pastor, he said, is already being out-preached by AI. Give Claude or GPT a Scripture passage and a congregation profile and it will produce a technically proficient sermon. It will cite commentaries. It will know the Greek. It will structure the argument better than most people who went to seminary. It will not miss the closing illustration.

What it cannot do is be known by the people sitting in the pews.

The irreplaceable thing is not competence. It is presence. The pastor who visited your house after the diagnosis. Who remembered the name of the dog. Who sat in the waiting room and did not check his phone. Who showed up. That is not something that can be automated, and it is also not something that gets measured in attendance reports or grant applications.

He was not arguing against technology. He was arguing for clarity about what the work actually is.

---

We talked about Kierkegaard for a while, which is not something that usually happens at Chick-fil-A in Jacksonville.

The argument was something like this: faith requires uncertainty. If you remove the uncertainty, you have not made faith stronger. You have replaced it with something else — certainty, maybe, or comfort, or a program. The uncertainty is not the problem faith has to overcome. The uncertainty is part of what faith is.

I told him about the farm. The fifteen months of unemployment. The diagnosis. The way everything came apart at the same time and how I had been trying to put it back together with different materials than the original. He received it. Not with a pastoral response, the kind trained to sound therapeutic. He just received it.

That is the thing about someone who has been doing campfire ministry long enough. They know how to be in a conversation without trying to fix it.

---

Jay Robinson runs the JSU Wesley Foundation in Jacksonville, Alabama. The campfire metaphor is his.
