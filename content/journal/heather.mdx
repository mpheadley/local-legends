I first saw her in music theory class at Jacksonville State.

I don't remember what she was wearing. I remember she had a pencil in her hand and she was writing something down and she wasn't paying attention to me at all, which was probably the beginning of the whole thing.

We got married. We had four kids — Hannah, Noah, Soren, Sage. We built a flower farm on nine acres near the Chief Ladiga Trail. We had sheep and a 1832 farmhouse and a little retail kiosk where she sold bouquets on Saturday mornings while the kids ran around in the grass.

She is a florist. A real one. She doesn't hand you a laminated menu — she asks what your grandmother grew in her backyard and builds something from there.

She took a converted horse trailer and turned it into the Bloom Bar: a mobile flower station where guests at weddings build their own arrangements to take home. The most photographed corner at every event she does. It was her idea. I had nothing to do with it.

<PullQuote>She was still there.</PullQuote>

In the fall of 2025, I was hospitalized. Ten days at UAB's psychiatric center.

She ran the farm sale while I was somewhere else.

I have thought about that a lot. The yard sale she ran alone. Moving through the equipment, the tools, four years of a life we'd built together. She did it while I was in a hospital room playing rummy and having my medication adjusted and trying to understand what had happened to me.

When I came home, we were in a camper on her parents' property.

She was still there.

I don't know what to do with that, theologically. There is probably a word for it in a language I don't speak. In English it just comes out as: she was still there.

---

She had endometriosis surgery before all of that. The recovery was long. She was barely out of bed when things started going wrong for me. She was the steady anchor before she was supposed to be well enough to be anything.

She is a type 2 on the Enneagram, which means she is wired to give what other people need. I have watched her try to learn how to also receive. It is the harder work, and she is doing it.

---

I wrote her a song. Not the recording — that's coming. But I found my journal with the lines already there, written in handwriting I barely recognized as mine.

*My rock, my flower. You save me, by the hour.*

I didn't remember writing it. But I had. Probably during some ordinary season when I wasn't paying close enough attention to ordinary seasons.

She was cutting stems when I found it. Six in the morning. Coffee going.

I showed it to her.

She read it. Didn't say anything for a minute.

Then she went back to the stems.

That's her.

The farm is gone. The business is still here. She books weddings and markets and installations and the Bloom Bar shows up at events and people take home flowers they built themselves and that is the thing she made that outlasted the acreage.
