The day I auditioned for the Men's Chorale at Jacksonville State, Dr. Corbin had me sing down the scale. I hit a D below the bass clef staff and he turned to the bass anchor, the man already seated in the chair I was about to need, and said: "Move over."

Jason Wright moved. He laughed. No complaint, no staked-out territory, no performance of generosity. He just made room and went back to being who he had always been.

That was twenty years ago.

---

Hadestown is the story of Orpheus and Eurydice. Orpheus descends into the underworld to try to bring his love back from the dead. He plays his way past the guards, past Hades, past the machinery of a place that runs on people selling their souls for the illusion of security. He is allowed to lead Eurydice back to the living world on one condition: he cannot look back.

He looks back.

The show is set in a kind of Depression-era American South. Hadestown is not hell exactly — it is an industrial hellhole, a factory town, a place where the desperate trade their freedom for the promise of warmth and a meal. Hades is not a monster. He is a man who has decided that fear is more reliable than love.

Jason Wright played Orpheus in a production of Hadestown. He played his own guitar — a PRS, live, onstage — because Orpheus plays guitar, and Jason is a tenor, and the role fit. He told me once, when we were talking about it, that there was a technique for the guitar: a wire run through the strings to get a specific rasping sound, almost electric but acoustic. He showed me.

He has since directed at the Piedmont Theater. Last year: Little Shop of Horrors. He has been on both sides of it — in the role, then running the room.

Anniston is not the underworld. But it is a city that has been told, in various ways, that its best days are behind it. A post-industrial small city in northeast Alabama, about which people in Birmingham have opinions. The kind of place where a regional theater matters the way a town square matters — it is where the community goes to hear itself think.

Jason has chosen to show up for it. He has not chosen easier stories.

---

We worked together for years at Jacksonville State — he was the chorale treasurer, the steady bass section, the man in the room who kept things humming without announcing that he was doing it — and then both of us ended up at Anniston First United Methodist Church. He directed worship. I was eventually his direct supervisor for four years. I would have called him a friend before that. What the years in a church building together made us is harder to name.

When I resigned in March 2025, I stopped reaching out to people. I was manic, though I would not know that for months. I thought I was better than ever. I resigned on Ash Wednesday and spent the next months in a kind of clarity that turned out to be psychosis.

During all of that, Jason called.

Not once. He checked on me regularly through months when I was not responding to anyone. I was hiding. He kept calling. He eventually stepped into the Worship Arts Director role I had left. He did it without making it a statement about anything. He just did the work.

---

Last summer, I built him a website. We sat down on July 1st and talked about it. He told me he felt honored and relieved.

Relieved was the word that stopped me. Not excited. Not impressed. Relieved. Like something had been done that he had been waiting for without knowing how to ask.

"We often try to be clever and to impress," he said. "And it's just coming from a place of fear and insecurity."

He was talking about how most people approach their public presence. But he was also naming the opposite of what it felt like to have something finally tell the truth about who you were.

I asked what that experience was like.

"You get the privilege of being known through your design," he said.

The privilege. Not the convenience. Not the benefit. The privilege.

---

When Jason played Orpheus, the music was the whole thing. Orpheus wins because he can play — the music is the argument, the reason the guards let him through, the reason Hades pauses, the reason Eurydice almost makes it back. Without the music, there is no story. There is just a man walking into the dark for nothing.

He has been making room and staying in the room for twenty years. That is not nothing. That is actually the whole thing.

Jason is a tenor. He moves when told to move. He stays.

---

*Jason Wright is the Worship Arts Director at Anniston First United Methodist Church and a director at the Piedmont Theater in Anniston.*
