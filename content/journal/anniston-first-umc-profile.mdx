Anniston First United Methodist Church sits at the corner of Noble Street and Thirteenth — a downtown block that has been through several versions of itself and keeps on being downtown.

The building is large by Anniston standards. The sanctuary holds more people than most Sundays fill it. The stained glass is old. The organ is real. The parking lot, like most downtown church parking lots, has become a constant negotiation between the congregation and whoever else needs a parking spot on Noble Street.

The church has been there for over a century. It has survived the century, which is not nothing.

---

Anniston First was formed when Anniston was still being formed. The city was a planned industrial town, built in the 1870s on the premise that a clean, organized settlement could anchor a new kind of Southern economy. It didn't fully work out that way, but Anniston stayed. Churches stayed with it. Anniston First grew with the city through the boom years and the bust years and the long stretch of years when the question about Northeast Alabama's future was more complicated than the boosters had hoped.

What the church became, over those decades, was the kind of institution that accumulates layers. Camp Lee is on the mountain — a church camp that AFUMC has operated for generations, where kids go in summer and churches go for retreats and adults go to figure out what they believe. The Arts and Wellness Center on Noble Street. Programs across a century of community need. A choir that has outlasted many of its members and keeps taking in new ones.

The current senior pastor is Tammy Jackson — a pastor with the kind of competence and warmth that makes a congregation feel held, and the kind of spine that makes a congregation feel led. She has been there through a complicated institutional season and has not blinked.

Kyle Bryan is the executive director of the Arts and Wellness Center. He runs the programs that fill the building during the week, the ones that reach into the community past Sunday morning.

---

The church also owns the forest.

That is what Camp Lee amounts to, for those who have been there: a forest on the mountain with cabins and a chapel and the specific silence that happens when the camp is between sessions. I have sat in that silence. I have been formed there. I know what the camp is in the life of the people who come back to it.

Institutions like Anniston First carry things that individual people cannot hold alone — the memory of what was done there, the record of who was present, the buildings that stay long after the people who built them have left. The camp is part of that. So is the building on Noble Street. So is whatever has been sustained in that place across the decade-by-decade decisions about whether to stay or sell or fold or try something different.

AFUMC kept making the decision to stay. That is not a small thing.

## MattNote

I worked for this church for nearly a decade. I was a pastoral intern, then an associate pastor, then eventually a direct supervisor for staff including people I deeply respected. I also resigned from this church in March 2025 during a manic episode I did not yet have the language for, and what followed was one of the hardest years of my life.

I cannot write about Anniston First objectively. I loved it there. I was also not well there, and not well in ways I did not understand until much later. The church did not fail me. I did not fail the church. What happened was that a person broke, and the institution had to keep going, and both of those things were true at the same time.

I still drive past the building. The sign is the same. Kyle still runs good programs. Tammy is the kind of pastor the congregation needs. Camp Lee is still on the mountain, which I think is the most important thing I could say.

What I could not earn my way back to, I had to learn to let go of. That is a whole other essay. But the church is real, and it is doing real work, and it deserves a record that is more than my complicated story of leaving it.

— Matt
