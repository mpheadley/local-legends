There is a church camp on the mountain called Camp Lee. Anniston First owns it. That's the church I resigned from, in the middle of a manic episode I didn't yet have a name for, one morning in Lent.

By last fall I wanted it back. Not the mania. The job. The twenty years. The life I had before I lit the match.

So I went to work at Camp Lee.

I want to be honest about why. I told myself it was work, and it was work, but underneath the work was a plan, and the plan was this: if I showed up, if I was useful, if I made myself small enough and steady enough for long enough, maybe someone would put in a word. Maybe the door would open again. I was forty-two years old with two decades of ministry behind me and a torn meniscus in my knee, and I had a plan to get my calling back by picking up rocks.

That is what the job was. I picked up stones and moved them from one place to another. I ran a leaf blower. They were building an obstacle course up there, a ropes course, and I built the platforms for it. Cut the boards, set the posts, made the structures the kids would climb on and swing from once the camp filled up in summer. Manual labor on a mountain I used to come to for something else entirely.

Because I had been to Camp Lee before. Years earlier I sat in a circle of chairs up there at a retreat with the Academy for Spiritual Formation, a day with nothing on the schedule but listening, and I learned the difference between a spiritual director and a spiritual companion. A director stands above the work and points. A companion walks alongside. I loved that camp. I had been formed there. Now I was the maintenance.

One day the staff came out to test the course.

I heard the trucks before I saw them. The people I used to sit in staff meetings with, the people whose names I still knew, came up the mountain to try out the thing I was building. I kept my head down and kept working and tried to make it not awkward. It was awkward. There is no version of that scene that isn't awkward. You cannot move rocks casually while the people who used to be your colleagues watch you move rocks.

Here is the detail that undoes me still. Back at the church, the office I was supposed to have, the one that came with the associate pastor title I had spent years fighting for, was now occupied by someone else. Somebody had my office. And I was outside, on the mountain, in the leaves, building a playground.

Nobody said any of this to me. Nobody was cruel. That's the thing I keep having to explain. There was no villain that day, no one who came out to humiliate me. The humiliation was structural. It was in the geography. They were inside where I thought I would be. I was outside with a leaf blower. The arrangement of bodies in space said the whole thing, and everyone was too kind to say it out loud, which somehow made it worse.

I felt foolish. I felt like a man who had misread his own life so badly that he was now trying to sweep his way back into it.

And it taught me the one thing I had come up the mountain to avoid learning. The door was closed. Not slammed. Closed. My relationship with that place had changed at the root, and no amount of usefulness was going to change it back. You cannot earn your way back into a room that has already given your chair to someone else. I could move every rock on that mountain and it would not move the one thing I wanted moved.

I worked there about two weeks.

I dropped it right before I went to the hospital.

That is the sentence I keep landing on. Camp Lee was the last thing I tried before I ran out of things to try. It sits in my memory right at the edge, the final attempt to find a way back, abandoned at the lip of the fall. After the rocks came the hospital. I did not know that yet, standing up there in the cold with the leaf blower, but some part of me must have. You do not keep moving rocks after you know they will not move anything. You stop. And then, if you are lucky, and I was lucky, something else catches you.

There is one more thing I know about Camp Lee now that I did not know that winter.

In September 2020, Kyle Bryan's daughter Lucy was killed in a car crash. She was three years old. She loved playgrounds. She would point out every one she saw from the car window.

Her funeral was held at the Martha Vandervoort Chapel at Camp Lee.

I moved rocks on that mountain while carrying something I could not name, in a place that had already held a different grief I did not know about. The mountain keeps things I did not put there. That is the thing about institutions. They accumulate what you bring and what everyone before you brought. You are never the only story on the property.

I have been to the bottom in a few different ways. People assume the bottom was the hospital, or the mania, or the farm we lost. Those were terrible. But the bottom, the real one, was quieter than any of that. The bottom was a mountain I loved, a leaf blower, an office that wasn't mine, and the slow understanding that no was a complete sentence and it had already been spoken.

I still drive past Camp Lee. The sign is the same. The road up is the same. I keep the windows up.
