Jacksonville had a farmers market for a while.

I am not sure exactly when it started. I know it was on the square, which is the right place for that kind of thing, and I know that on Saturday mornings in the warmer months there would be tables and tents and people selling tomatoes and okra and honey and sometimes soap, and people from Jacksonville would come out and walk the square and buy things or not buy things and mostly just be near each other.

I went more than once when I was living there. I was at Jacksonville State. My world was the campus and the Wesley Foundation and the rehearsal rooms in Mason Hall and a few apartments I kept visiting because the people in them were interesting. The farmers market on the square was outside all of that, which was the point. You went to be in Jacksonville rather than to be in your particular pocket of it.

---

The square is the kind of space that only works if people keep choosing it. Jacksonville's is not large. The courthouse is there. A few storefronts of varying survival. Parking on the perimeter. What makes a courthouse square a gathering place is not the infrastructure. It is the decision, renewed by enough people on enough mornings, that this is worth doing.

The market made that decision visible.

You could see who was choosing to be there. The farmer who had driven in from outside town with the truck bed full of whatever the week had produced. The older woman who had made pepper jelly and was selling it in small labeled jars. The couple who came every week and bought the same things. The person who showed up for the first time and walked around twice, unsure what to get, and eventually bought some tomatoes and a jar of honey, and that was enough.

---

I am from this county, by which I mean I have been here in several forms across several decades. JSU student. Married young in the chapel across from the football stadium. Left. Came back. The market on the square is one of those things I think about when I think about what Jacksonville actually is, underneath the institutional version — the university town, the county seat, the logistics of it.

What it actually is, or can be, is a place where people show up on a Saturday with something to offer, and other people show up to receive it, and for a few hours the square does exactly what squares were built to do.

I am not sure the market is still running in the form I remember it. Things change. Markets start and stop. The people who made it go eventually stop making it go and nobody else picks it up right away.

But it happened. It was a real thing. People grew things and brought them into town and sold them to their neighbors, and that is not nothing. That is actually something close to everything.
