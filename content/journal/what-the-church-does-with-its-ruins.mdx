William Christenberry drove back to Hale County, Alabama every summer for forty years to photograph the same abandoned buildings. A church. A Coca-Cola sign going to rust. A Klan-owned store he couldn't stop looking at even though, maybe because, he found it morally unbearable. He kept going back. The buildings kept decaying. He kept going back anyway.

I spent seven years as a pastor in Northeast Alabama. I know what the church does with its ruins. It takes them down. It builds something newer in their place, or it sells the property and dissolves the congregation and mails the last members a letter with a Scripture verse in the closing. It moves on. What it rarely does is stand in front of the thing that fell and ask what it meant that it fell.

Southern Gothic art does the opposite. It drives back every summer.

There's a reason the genre keeps returning to abandoned churches specifically. Not just abandoned barns or empty storefronts, churches. The church is the institution the South asked to hold its conscience, and the church held it in complicated ways, and now the buildings sit empty on red clay roads while kudzu covers the steps, and the artists won't stop photographing them. Flannery O'Connor spent her whole career writing about what happens when the spirit leaves the building but the institution stays open. The building eventually follows.

I was ordained in two denominations. I led worship on Sunday mornings while my own interior was coming apart. I sat in a pew at AFUMC one Sunday after leading the service and listened to a colleague preach about a mental health center downtown and thought: *Is that my fate? Will I end up there?* I did not say it out loud. I kept my face pastoral and went home.

Two years later I called 988 from my room at the farm and spent ten days in a psychiatric unit at UAB. The church I had served was one of the institutions that did not know what to do with that information.

I'm not angry about it anymore. But I notice it.

Southern Gothic keeps noticing what the church would prefer to leave unexamined. O'Connor's preachers are frauds or fools or both. The violence in her stories comes from people trying to outrun a grace they can feel pressing against the back of their necks. In *The Devil All the Time*, Robert Pattinson plays a traveling preacher who is running the same con as the congregation, everyone is pretending, and the pretending is what makes it lethal. In Ethel Cain's *Preacher's Daughter*, the whole album is a daughter trying to get out from under a theology that loved her conditionally and damaged her permanently. It is one of the most theologically honest things I have heard in years, and it came from a queer woman from Tallahassee who left the church, not from anyone who stayed.

This is what Instagram gets wrong about the South and what Southern Gothic gets right. Instagram gives you the beautiful church on the hill, the white clapboard, the stained glass lit from inside. It gives you the aesthetic without the history. Southern Gothic gives you the same church forty years later with the windows broken and the hymnals still on the pews, and it asks you to sit with the question of what happened between those two photographs.

I sat in those buildings for seven years. I know what happened.

The tradition produced real beauty and real damage in roughly equal measure, and the damage is not less real because the beauty was genuine. That's the thing the grotesque in Southern Gothic is trying to say, not that the church was all lie, but that you cannot have the beauty without accounting for what it cost. Grace, in O'Connor's work, always comes at the price of something. It arrives like a collision.

I've been thinking about what it means to write religion commentary from inside this tradition. Not from outside the church as a scorned exile, and not from inside it as an apologist. From the position of someone who was ordained, served faithfully, broke open, was rebuilt, and is now standing in front of the ruins asking what they mean.

Christenberry didn't hate Hale County. He kept going back because he loved it and couldn't look away from what it contained. That's the only honest position I know.

The buildings are still there. The kudzu is still growing. The question is whether we drive back.
