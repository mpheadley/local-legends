Kyle Bryan preached a sermon about going to the hospital. He called it a vacation with grippy socks.

I heard about it before I met him. The phrase traveled the way phrases do when they unlock something. The socks with the rubber on the bottom — the kind they give you when they don't trust you with the other kind. He named it from a pulpit. He did not perform the recovery. He did not ask the congregation to feel a certain way about it. He just said: this happened, here is what it looked like, and here I am.

That was the sermon.

Years later, I became the associate pastor at Anniston First United Methodist Church, and Kyle Bryan had been the associate pastor before me. He had left in good standing to run the United Way homeless shelter next door. Not a metaphor. The shelter is literally next door to the church. You can see it from the parking lot.

---

Kyle serves as Executive Pastor at Anniston First now. He has been connected to that building — as associate pastor, as shelter director, as the man who stayed — long enough that it would be hard to know where the institution ends and he begins.

His writing tells you who he is faster than a bio will.

He published an essay this month on his Substack, *The Common Table*, about the closing of Martha's Hope. The shelter had served hundreds of people. More than 20,000 nights of safety. More than 40,000 meals. It closed. Kyle's essay is not a complaint. It is a prophetic indictment, written without a raised voice.

He has the data. He has the names: the volunteers, the sheriff's deputies, the healthcare professionals, the public officials who showed courage. He does not swing at anyone. He names what happened and then names what it means.

Then he signs his name to it. Executive Pastor, Anniston First United Methodist Church. He puts his role and his institution's name directly under a critique of the community they both serve.

That takes something. I am not sure what to call it except: knowing the truth is load-bearing. You can write it because you are not afraid of it. You are not afraid of it because you have already said harder things, from a pulpit, about yourself.

He had already preached the sermon. He had already said it out loud: the place they send you when you are out of options is survivable. He said it before he needed to. That is the order of operations for a particular kind of person. Words first. Then presence.

---

The Martha's Hope essay ends this way:

<PullQuote>History eventually strips away our explanations and leaves only our choices.</PullQuote>

He is not writing about himself when he writes that. He is writing about a community. But the sentence is also the kind of sentence that comes from someone who has sat with their own choices long enough to know they are what remain.

The shelter next door is closed. Kyle is still next door. He chose, years ago, to move from pastoral ministry to direct service — to be in proximity to the people who do not get to go home after the service. Then the institution asked him back. He came back.

The grippy socks are not the point.

The getting back up is.

---

In September 2020, Kyle and Leann Bryan were driving with their two children when a Mustang crossed the center line on Leon Smith Parkway in Oxford and hit their minivan head-on. Their daughter Lucy was three years old. She was airlifted to Children's Hospital in Birmingham. She did not survive.

Lucy Sophia Bryan was born April 19, 2017. She died September 10, 2020.

She loved playgrounds. She would point out every one she saw from the car window.

The memorial playground built in her name is at The Children's Place daycare at Anniston First United Methodist Church — the building Kyle has served his whole adult life. Every child who runs through it is running through something her parents chose to give away when they had nothing left to give.

Lucy's funeral was held at the Martha Vandervoort Chapel at Camp Lee. On the mountain. The same mountain. The same camp that AFUMC owns, where Kyle's congregation goes for retreats, where the summer programs run every year. That is where they buried her service.

I did not know this the winter I worked at Camp Lee moving rocks, trying to earn my way back into a room that had already given my chair to someone else. I know it now. The mountain holds more than I was paying attention to.

Kyle kept showing up after that. He wrote about homelessness. He ran the shelter. He came back to executive ministry when they asked. He preached about the hospital. He came to see me when I was in one.

He has earned the right to say hard things quietly. He paid for it.
