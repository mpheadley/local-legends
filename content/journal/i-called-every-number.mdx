I was in my room at the farmhouse when I called 988.

This was before the hospital. Before the ten days at UAB. Before the camper in my parents' backyard. I called the number because I did not know what else to do.

The person on the other end was kind. She asked if I was safe. I said I was trying to be. She stayed on the line for a while. When we hung up, I was still standing in the field, but I knew I needed more than a phone call. I needed to know what was actually available in Calhoun County. Who to call. Where to go. Whether anyone would answer.

So I searched.

---

The first result was a state page. An address list, last updated nobody could tell me when. Three phone numbers. I called the first one. It rang to a fax machine.

I tried the second. Disconnected.

The third connected to a general intake line for a program that had closed. The woman at the number was kind, too. She said they used to refer people to a peer support group that met on Tuesdays. She was not sure if it still met. She had not checked.

I am a pastor. I was an associate pastor at Anniston First United Methodist for four years. I had an office on the same campus as Interfaith Ministries. I had sat across from Randy in that parking lot for years — Randy in his wheelchair, gentle, soft-spoken, once a hair stylist — and I had watched him disappear one day and learned months later that he had died. I knew the names. I knew the gaps. And even I could not find the resources when I needed them.

That is the thing about a mental health crisis. You do not go into it with research time.

---

After the hospital, after the camper, after coming home, I started thinking about what I could not find.

I am not a clinician. I have a Master of Divinity, not a license. I am not qualified to tell you which therapist is best for your particular situation. But I can call a number and find out if it rings. I can verify who is accepting new patients. I can ask which programs still meet, and when, and where.

So I built a list.

It took two weeks to verify the first twenty entries. Crisis lines. Therapists in Calhoun, Etowah, and St. Clair counties who are currently accepting patients and take Medicaid. The peer support group that still meets on Tuesday nights, in the basement of a church on Quintard. The faith-based counseling programs that the national directories do not index because they run out of local churches and do not pay thirty dollars a month to Psychology Today.

I called every number on the list. Not all of them answered. I marked the ones that did.

---

Highland Health Systems has a crisis line. It works. Craig Crisis Care runs a mobile crisis response team, twenty-four hours. The number is real. 988 is real and they will stay on the line.

Those are the ones you need at two in the morning when you are standing in the field.

The others — the therapists, the peer groups, the counselors in church offices, the community programs — those are the ones you need the week after, when the crisis has passed and you are trying to figure out what comes next.

Calhoun County has more than you think. It has less than it needs. The map is not complete. It may never be complete.

But it exists now, and I am keeping it current.

---

If you are in Calhoun County and you need help, the list is at [gatherstudio.app/steady](https://gatherstudio.app/steady).

If you know of a resource in NE Alabama that should be on it, I want to hear from you.

If you called a number on the list and it rang to a fax machine, tell me. I will fix it that week.

You are not the first person in this county to feel this way. You will not be the last. The names on that list are people who will pick up.
