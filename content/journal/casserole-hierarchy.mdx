[**MATT WRITES: Opening scene.** A specific fellowship hall. The smell, the table, what was on it, who was there. One paragraph, scene over summary. Drop us in the room.]

In the United Methodist Church, the potluck is not a meal. It is a governance document.

Every dish on that folding table is a vote. For what, nobody agrees. That's the point.

There is a taxonomy. Nobody has written it down because writing it down would be vulgar, which is exactly the kind of thing people in the fellowship hall care about. You learn it the way you learn everything at church: by watching who gets thanked from the pulpit and who doesn't.

**Tier One: The Casseroles.**

Not all casseroles. The right casseroles. Green bean with French's onions on top. Chicken and rice, covered. These arrive in Pyrex because their mothers arrived in Pyrex. The Pyrex is the credential. It says: I have been doing this long enough that my dish has survived several hundred trips through the dishwasher and is fine.

A casserole in a foil pan is a different statement. It says the cook can't commit, or is new, or bought something and needed the anonymity of a container they can leave behind. Nobody says this. You learn it by watching.

**Tier Two: Deviled Eggs.**

The deviled egg is the most politically complex food in the fellowship hall. I am not exaggerating.

There are women in this congregation who have been making deviled eggs for forty years. They have a method. They don't write it down. They don't share it. If you ask, they'll tell you it's just the regular recipe. They're lying.

The problem is that deviled eggs are finite and immediately visible. There are never enough. The women who made them know exactly how many they made. They're counting. They see who took two. They've noted it.

Getting to the deviled eggs first is not greed. It is theological urgency. Don't let anyone tell you otherwise.

**Tier Three: The Desserts.**

A layer cake made from scratch sits at the top. A sheet cake from the Piggly Wiggly sits at the bottom. Between these poles lies every human motivation: the woman who bakes when she's anxious, the man who has brought his mother's pound cake recipe for twenty years and is afraid to change now, the child who convinced her grandmother to let her carry the Jell-O mold.

The Jell-O mold exists outside the taxonomy. It is not Tier Three. It is not any tier. It is the ecumenical challenge of the fellowship hall: you find a way to include it. You don't say anything about it. It's been here longer than you have.

**Tier Four: Everything Else.**

Vegetable trays. Rolls from a bag. Grocery store cookies arranged on a platter to look homemade, which fools no one.

These are accepted without comment. Grace is extended. But grace is not the same as honor.

---

I was a pastor for nineteen years. I have attended more potlucks than I have preached sermons, and I have preached a lot of sermons. I have eaten green bean casserole in fellowship halls that smelled like burnt coffee and carpet cleaner. I have stood at the end of the buffet line, surveying the landscape, and calculated.

I have also, at a church I won't name, watched the same woman bring the same dish for thirty years.

Not the same type of dish. The same dish. Same casserole. Same Pyrex. Same foil crimped the same way. Her husband used to carry it in from the car. He died and after that she carried it herself.

I asked her about it once, the way you ask people at church about things you already know the answer to.

She said she liked making it.
