The building is a small white house with blue shutters.

There's a wooden ramp up to the front door, painted red. Out front, a white sign on two posts: *Jacksonville Christian Outreach Center, 256-435-1891*. Big trees behind it. The kind of setup that looks, from the road, like it might be a church annex or a dentist's old office. You could drive past it without noticing.

Summer Jennings has worked there for fourteen years. She walked in to help and ended up staying.

"I got finagled into it," she told me.

---

The Jacksonville Christian Outreach Center has been doing this since 1996. Thirty years this year. For three decades, on three days a week, four hours at a time, they have opened the door to people in Calhoun County who need food, utility help, school supplies, care bags for people walking the streets with nowhere to go.

Summer is the only paid staff member.

She runs grants, scheduling, operations, the treasury. She also runs ChatGPT — she calls it JC — for every flyer, grant application, and report the organization produces. She keeps ten sets of keys distributed to volunteers she trusts. She coordinates with Walmart, Dollar General, Target, Community Food Bank, United Way, two food banks, four churches, JSU graphic design students, a diaper bank in Birmingham, and a garden that just started planting this spring.

I asked her how she didn't burn out.

"It's not nine to five," she said. "And it doesn't feel repetitive."

---

Walk through the front and into the pantry and the shelves go floor to ceiling on both sides of a narrow aisle. Canned goods, dry goods, cereals, snacks. At the far end, a commercial refrigerator — Walmart donated it when the produce started coming in volume. Summer was in there when I arrived, sunglasses pushed up on her head, teal JCOC shirt, arm in the air like she was flagging someone down.

Last year the center received 63,000 pounds of donated retail food. Walmart joined in August — two pickups a week, up to 700 pounds at a time. The Community Food Bank delivers 3,000 pounds a month. Dollar General keeps them stocked on milk. Target sends healthy snacks and personal hygiene products.

"There's no way we would ever buy these," Summer said, holding up a flavored drink the kids apparently love. "We try to be as good of students as we can be."

She meant stewards. The slip made sense anyway.

---

The programs have names that somebody worked to make human. Toyland. Sunny Bites. Little Needs. The logos came from JSU graphic design students — the kind of collaboration that happens when a small nonprofit figures out what a university student needs (a real project with real stakes) and what they need (free creative work that looks like it matters). The logos are good.

Toyland is the Christmas toy giveaway — long folding tables in a borrowed fellowship hall, covered end to end with boxed dolls and games and action figures. Sunny Bites runs in summer, partnering with Calhoun County schools and the Community Food Bank to feed a hundred families with kids eighteen and under during the months when school lunch disappears. There's a new diaper bank partnership with a Birmingham organization called Bundles of Hope.

The garden just started. A woman named Summer Angel — not a title, an actual name — is running it, funded sixty-five dollars a year by the supporting churches. The food will be available to anyone. Not just clients.

---

Summer grew up Catholic, spent time in a Baptist church, now attends Jacksonville First United Methodist. Her son is a music major at JSU, in four bands, playing drum corps in Atlanta, on the president's list.

She said all of that without irony, the way people talk about things that are simply true.

The previous director left. Summer was already there. The job became hers.

"Baby steps," she said. "But one day I see it not being like this."

She meant the building, the constraints, the house with its awkward additions and the shelves that go right up to the ceiling because there isn't anywhere else to put things. She did not mean the work. The work she seemed to like fine.

---

Thirty years of this. One paid staff person. A small white house with blue shutters on a street in Jacksonville, Alabama.

The tagline on the website is "Helping when life hurts." Summer didn't overthink it. That's just what they do.
