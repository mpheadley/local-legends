In the spring of 2016 I ran a race in Woodstock, Alabama, pushing a double running stroller with both of my twins inside.

Sage and Soren were small. The kind of small where they fit in the stroller without argument, where they were still at the age of riding while adults moved and they would just look at whatever was passing and be content. They did not know this was a race. They did not know I was trying to prove something. They were passengers.

I knew. I was trying to prove something.

---

I am not sure exactly what the race was. A 5K, I think, or something close to it. Woodstock is a community in Bibb County, south of Birmingham — a small place with a long memory, the kind of Alabama town that exists mostly as a location people are from. The race was the kind of thing that places like that put on to give people a reason to gather.

I showed up with the double stroller and two small children and whatever I was carrying that morning.

That spring, 2016, Heather and I had four kids. Hannah was nine. Noah was eight. And then the twins, newly three. Sage and Soren, same age, different people, both riding in a stroller while their father pushed them through a road race in a town none of us lived in.

I have been asked more than once why I did this. The honest answer is that I do not fully know. Some of it was that I had been running, and running was the thing I could do to feel like I was still doing something. Some of it was that taking the twins on an outing felt like it counted for something. And some of it was that there is a certain kind of man who will push a double stroller through a road race on a Saturday morning and feel that he has accomplished something, even if he is slower than everyone without a stroller, even if both kids fall asleep before the finish line, even if nobody asked him to do this.

I was that man. I am still that man. I contain him.

---

The *Anniston Star* was there. They photographed the race. I know this because I have seen the picture.

I am in it. Stroller in front of me, both twins in it, face forward, pushing. It is the face of a person who has decided something and is not going to be talked out of it.

That is the whole thing, actually. The whole photo. The whole race.

I crossed the finish line. The twins were fine. I was tired.

Heather was somewhere nearby, I think, or heard about it later. She has learned over the years that certain things I do are best processed after rather than during. Pushing a double stroller through a road race in Bibb County is in that category.

---

Sage is ten now. Soren is ten. They would not remember the stroller or the race or the finish line in Woodstock. It lives only in my memory and in the *Anniston Star* archive and in whatever this piece adds to it.

Which is: they were there. They were small and they were riding and I was pushing them toward something I couldn't have named. That seems about right for being their dad. I hope they know it eventually. I hope they find it worth something that I was determined even when I was being a little stupid about what the determination was for.

That is the stroller story. That is the Woodstock race.

That is 2016.

---

*Matt Headley has been running in Northeast Alabama for most of his adult life. Sage and Soren are now ten. They do not ride in strollers anymore. [Southern Legends.](/)*
