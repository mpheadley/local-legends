I started getting 5-gallon buckets of coffee grounds from Called Coffee for my flower farm. Not exactly how most customer relationships begin.

But that's Jerod.

In 2021, I watched him gut the old Daylight Donuts building on Quintard, two blocks from the Methodist church where I'd just taken a job, and rebuild it into something the town had been missing for years. The gutting. The rebuilding. The vision taking shape in real time. We were both from Weaver, both working toward something in Anniston, and we barely knew it yet.

When Called Coffee finally opened, I became a regular. The smell hit you first: beans getting roasted by the sackful, that rich aroma that means someone's doing the work right. And I brought my buckets.

Those coffee ground runs gave us time to talk. Jerod supported our flower farm early, let us display mason jar arrangements in the shop, used our bud vases on the tables. When things got hard for me personally, he checked in. Kept up with me. Showed up.

The story behind the coffee starts in Peru.

In 2016, Jerod and his wife Hannah sold everything and moved to Huancayo with their eight-month-old daughter to plant a church. A church member named Elisa mentioned, almost offhandedly, that her father Gregorio grew coffee on a highland farm. Jerod tasted it. "At that time, it was the best coffee I had ever had." He had no idea what to do with that. Neither did Gregorio. "We just agreed to help them try to get it to the U.S. And I had no idea how to do that."

He figured it out. When Jerod met Gregorio, the farmer was selling his beans for roughly 70 cents a pound. By 2025, Called Coffee was paying him around $4.50 a pound and sending a bonus on top. "This last year," Jerod told me, "the bonus was more than what we paid for the coffee." Gregorio's son, who once hated the coffee business, now runs his own shop in the city. They're building a new house. A lower-yield season this year, too much rain, but Jerod's still buying.

He just connected with a farmer in Honduras doing the same work under the same terms. "It looks like we're gonna be able to help him just as much as we help Gregorio."

None of it is comfortable. That's the part people miss when they romanticize the third space, the drive-through, the small-town coffee shop with the good story. "I think every small business until you hit a certain point is right at the brink of failure," Jerod said. "Another COVID comes, someone gets sick in your family. It's very fragile." Coffee prices tripled in 2024 with no warning. The margins in food and beverage leave almost no cushion. "I'm not in any shape or form comfortable and set for life, or even the next month if things go south."

On the hard days, he calls Gregorio's son in Peru. "I just chatted with him, and it got me motivated again. This is why I'm doing this."

He describes entrepreneurship as a roller coaster running on a ten-minute loop: this is great, this is terrible, this is great, this is terrible. You have to be able to tolerate it. Not everybody can. His wife helps keep him balanced, "or I guess my guilt of what I put my wife through helps keep me balanced," he said, which got a laugh but landed like a confession.

His daughter is named Selah. סֶלָה. The word appears 74 times in the Psalms, usually untranslated, marking a pause between stanzas. A breath. An interlude before the next thing begins. A man who names his daughter Selah and then builds a place where strangers rest and exhale and reconnect — I don't think that's an accident. She's coming up in the business. "I feel sorry for her competitors," he said. "She's cold-blooded."

The employees are part of the mission too. Four of them are using a Chamber-negotiated deal: 20% off at JSU, buy-one-get-one classes at Gadsden State. "That's motivating," he said. "That's a killer deal."

I sat by the big window at a small table more times than I can count. Working alone, meeting friends, meeting new ones. One afternoon I watched a guy I'd never met strike up a conversation with someone at the next table over croissants and cold brew. By the time I left, they were exchanging business cards. That's what happens when you build a space where people feel like they belong.

Jerod didn't plan for any of this. Not the community garden they're still sketching out. Not becoming a community hub. Not the change in downtown Anniston over the last four years. "The community has really embraced that," he said. "It wasn't on my priority list."

He started with a bag of beans and a church member's father's farm in the Peruvian highlands.

"I was just trying to sell a bag. Still don't know what's next."
