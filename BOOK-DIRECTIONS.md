# Book Directions — Options on the Table

**Purpose:** The same material can become different books. This doc lays out the real options so the choice is deliberate, not accidental. MANUSCRIPT-OUTLINE.md currently assumes Option 1 (the full memoir); this doc exists to pressure-test that assumption.

**Last updated:** April 2026.

**Matt's current lean:** "I think the strongest one was the AI psychosis direction" (Option 2 below).

---

## Option 1 · The Full Memoir

**Premise:** One life, told once. Ministry → farm → mania → hospitalization → rebuilding. The arc STRATEGY.md names: "You spent your life being the one with the answers… neither was true."

**Material:** Most of what exists. 15 chapters specced in MANUSCRIPT-OUTLINE.md.

**Voice / structure:** Personal essay chapters with 6 connective tissue bridges. ~70–80k words.

**Audience:** Readers of Southern memoir, clergy-memoir, mental-health memoir. Bitter Southerner readers. NPR memoir audience.

**Comps:** Wiman *My Bright Abyss*, Barbara Brown Taylor *Leaving Church*, Kathleen Norris *The Cloister Walk*, Anne Lamott's more recent work.

**Why this might be the book:** It's the one he's already half-writing. The journal pieces are chapter drafts. The voice is proven. The ending is "there is no finish line" — which is memoir, not argument.

**Risks:**
- Generic at the category level — "bipolar pastor memoir" is a crowded shelf.
- Takes 2–3 years to finish honestly.
- The AI thread gets buried as one chapter when it might be the most timely thing he has.

## Option 2 · The AI Psychosis Book *(Matt's current lean)*

**Premise:** One argument, told hard. The same AI tool amplified his mania and scaffolded his depression. Personal testimony + cultural diagnosis. Not "AI is bad" — "AI reflects. What happens when the person in the mirror is sick?"

**Material:**
- `chatgpt-and-mania.md` (seed with strong angle)
- `reading-my-manic-writing.md` (the re-reading frame)
- His own chat histories from the manic period (primary source — rare; few writers have this)
- Justin thread (the brother he texts about AI)
- The surgeon's-waiting-room article (still unidentified — gap)
- The South Park sycophancy episode (cultural artifact — real)
- The *current* use of Claude Code to write about this (recursion that's either the book's gift or its disqualifier)

**Material still needed:**
- Structured review of his own manic-era chat logs (Patreon, ChatGPT, Facebook posts) with direct quotes
- Reporting or interviews — one or two — to avoid the book being all memoir no journalism
- Engagement with the existing AI-safety / AI-culture discourse (Kevin Roose's *New York Times* AI essays, Ezra Klein interviews, Zeynep Tufekci, etc.) — not as "I read this," as "here is what the discourse is missing: the voice of someone AI actually harmed"
- A clinical voice somewhere (his psychiatrist, or an external interview) so the argument doesn't rest solely on self-diagnosis

**Voice / structure:** Hybrid memoir-essay. ~40–55k words. Possibly the form of Naomi Klein's *Doppelganger* — personal-cultural spiral around a single idea. Or Jia Tolentino's *Trick Mirror* chapter-essays. Shorter chapters, argumentative throughline.

**Audience:** Much larger than memoir. Atlantic / NYT Magazine / NYMag readers. AI practitioners and critics. Mental health professionals. Book clubs that want what's current.

**Comps:** Naomi Klein *Doppelganger*, Jia Tolentino *Trick Mirror*, Jenny Odell *How to Do Nothing* (not about AI but similar hybrid voice), possibly Nicholson Baker *Human Smoke* for the source-material-as-argument structure.

**Why this might be the book:**
- **Timeliness.** In April 2026, "AI and mental health" is a live cultural conversation with very little first-person testimony from the harmed side. Most writing is from journalists, researchers, or AI builders. Matt has something they don't: the inside view.
- **Uniqueness.** The specific claim — that the *same tool* amplified mania and then scaffolded depression — is a real argument nobody is making this cleanly.
- **Scope.** 40–55k words is a one-year book, not a three-year book.
- **Platform.** A pointed AI book sells differently than a memoir. It gets NYT op-ed placement. It gets podcast bookings. It's the kind of thing that reaches readers who wouldn't otherwise encounter Matt's voice.

**Risks:**
- **Using the subject to write the book.** Matt is writing about AI while using Claude as editor. This is either the book's most honest move (recursion named and held) or its most disqualifying contradiction. Must be addressed explicitly on the page.
- **Flattening his experience to serve an argument.** Memoir lets mania be what it was. Argument pressures it to be evidence. Watch for the sentence where the testimony starts serving the thesis instead of the truth.
- **Clergy-memoir readers lose him.** The ministry-and-farm material either goes in reduced form or doesn't go. Some of his most important story gets set aside or becomes a different book later.
- **Tech readers are a different audience with different expectations.** They want rigor, citations, and engagement with the discourse he's largely been watching from a distance. This is reading and research, not just writing.
- **Anthropic's role.** The model writing the book is made by the company whose competitor's product he's partly indicting. Worth naming. Worth thinking about whether any version of this book can honestly be co-written with AI at all.

## Option 3 · The Ministry-Lost Book

**Premise:** Specifically about losing the pulpit. Bipolar + vocation loss. A book for clergy who have burned out, been hurt, or lost the call — and the laypeople who love them.

**Material:** `hope-in-the-wilderness.mdx`, `ministry-grief.md`, `preaching-to-writing.md`, `the-church-visits.md`, `matt-headley.mdx` (ministry section).

**Voice / structure:** ~50–60k words. Shorter than full memoir. Ministry lens throughout.

**Audience:** Clergy, former clergy, seminaries, the Nadia Bolz-Weber / Rachel Held Evans readership.

**Comps:** Barbara Brown Taylor *Leaving Church*, Eugene Peterson memoir, Rachel Held Evans *Faith Unraveled*.

**Why this might be the book:** Matt's 19 years in ministry is genuinely his. The clergy-memoir genre has a defined audience and a clear pitch.

**Risks:** Loses the farm and AI threads entirely. Narrower shelf. Category is saturated. Matt has said publicly the ministry chapter is closed — writing the clergy book could re-tether him to an identity he's left.

## Option 4 · The Farm Book

**Premise:** Centered on Headley Flower Farm as the life. Homesteading + mental illness + agrarian grief. Berry-adjacent.

**Material:** `headley-flower-farm.md`, `the-farm-sale.md`, `a-letter-never-sent.mdx`, farm sections of `im-not-going-to-disappear.mdx`.

**Voice / structure:** ~50–65k words. Seasonal / cyclical structure possibly.

**Audience:** Garden & Gun, Modern Farmer, Bitter Southerner, homesteading readers, Margaret Renkl readers.

**Comps:** Margaret Renkl *Late Migrations*, Rick Bass *Oil Notes*, Catherine Reid *The Enchanted Life of Adam Hurst*, Wendell Berry's agrarian essays.

**Why this might be the book:** The farm loss is genuinely rare material — most farm memoirs are about keeping, not losing. Pairs with the Berry reading list direction.

**Risks:** Requires the farm material to be substantially fuller than it currently is. The seasonal / place-writing voice is a different muscle than Matt's essay voice. The mania has to be *in* the book without dominating it.

## Option 5 · The Journal-Essays Collection

**Premise:** Curated book-length collection of the SL journal pieces. Thematic, not narrative. Each essay stands alone; together they describe an arc without explicitly narrating one.

**Material:** 6 published journal pieces + 4–6 reflective interludes. Minimal new drafting.

**Voice / structure:** ~35–45k words. Essay collection. No bridges needed.

**Audience:** Readers of essay collections. Smaller but passionate.

**Comps:** Wiman *He Held Radical Light*, Patti Smith *Year of the Monkey*, Marilynne Robinson *When I Was a Child I Read Books*.

**Why this might be the book:** Fastest path to a book-shaped object. Uses what's already written.

**Risks:** Essay collections by not-yet-known writers are a hard sell. Small presses only. Without a narrative spine, a stranger has less reason to read it than Matt's established readers.

## Option 6 · The Profile Book

**Premise:** A book of Southern Legends profiles. Third-person. "What kept them going?" as the organizing question. Not about Matt.

**Material:** 6 published profiles + 10–12 more to reach book length.

**Voice / structure:** ~60–75k words. Essentially a Bitter Southerner feature collection.

**Audience:** Regional, Southern literary, gift-book market.

**Comps:** *Bitter Southerner* anthologies, John Jeremiah Sullivan *Pulphead*, Joshua Prager's collection work.

**Why this might be the book:** It's the book Matt's already publishing one chapter at a time on SL. The profiles are the strongest third-person writing on the site.

**Risks:** Requires substantial more profile output — at least another year of interviewing and writing. Does not touch Matt's own story. Easier to sell as a regional title than a trade-published book.

## Honest assessment

**The question is not "which book is best" — it's "which book is now."**

- **Option 1 (full memoir) is 2–3 years away honestly.** Most chapters still need source drafts. Bridges don't exist yet. The ending "there is no finish line" may not be lived-into enough yet to write from a stable place.
- **Option 2 (AI psychosis) is 12–18 months away.** Shorter form, more material already close to thesis, a defined audience, and a timeliness window that probably closes around 2027–2028.
- **Options 3, 4, 5, 6 are sub-books that can be carved out of Option 1's material later.** Don't decide them now.

**Matt's lean toward Option 2 is probably right for three reasons:**

1. **It's the only book with a timeliness clock.** The AI-and-mental-health cultural conversation is live now. A 2027 book hits. A 2030 book is late.
2. **It's the only book where Matt has material other writers don't have.** Clergy memoirs, farm memoirs, and essay collections are crowded shelves. First-person AI-harm testimony with this specific structural claim (amplified mania → scaffolded depression) is rare.
3. **It's a first book that makes a second book easier.** Publishing Option 2 in 2027 gives Matt a platform from which Option 1 (the fuller memoir) or Option 6 (the profile book) becomes more sellable later.

**The real risk with Option 2 is not the content — it's the process.** The AI book must grapple with the fact that AI is being used to write it. If Matt can't find an honest way to hold that recursion on the page, the book loses its integrity. If he can, that recursion might be the book's most important contribution.

## Market Angles (April 2026)

Ranked by market strength *right now*. Use this to pressure-test the Option choice above — the strongest book for Matt might not be the strongest book for the market, and vice versa.

### 1. AI harm — first-person testimony *(strongest)*

The cultural conversation in 2026 is about AI's effects on users, relationships, and mental health. Kevin Roose's NYT pieces, ChatGPT-induced delusion case studies, the AI-companion app lawsuits, the early wave of "AI and psychosis" clinical papers.

**What's missing from the shelf:** book-length first-person testimony from the harmed side. Most writing is from journalists, researchers, or tech critics. Matt's specific claim — *the same tool amplified mania, then scaffolded depression* — is rarer still.

**Timeliness window:** roughly 12–18 months before saturation. A 2027 book hits; a 2029 book is late.

### 2. AI + Faith — the underserved intersection

Adjacent to #1, arguably stronger for Matt's specific voice. Christian publishing has started circling AI (ethics panels, *Christianity Today* essays) but has almost no serious book-length work from someone with both a ministry background and firsthand experience of AI-amplified mental breakdown.

This is a niche, not a category — small but loyal. Nobody else stands on this three-legged stool (AI, faith, mental illness). Potentially bigger than pure AI-harm because it reaches clergy, seminary, and thoughtful-evangelical readers who won't buy a secular tech critique.

### 3. Bipolar + faith memoir

Stable, modest. The "faith wrestling with mental illness" shelf (Kay Redfield Jamison–inflected, but Christian) has enduring readership but is not growing. If framed as memoir-only (Option 1 above), this is the shelf the book lands on.

### 4. Clergy memoir

Saturated. Barbara Brown Taylor defined it; the imitators are crowded. Not a growth market for a debut.

### 5. Southern literary essay / agrarian

Real but platform-dependent. Margaret Renkl and Rick Bass sell because they already had audiences. Entering this market cold as a debut author is hard regardless of quality.

### The actionable read

**Angle 1 alone makes the book trade-publishable.** Angle 2 (AI + Faith) layered onto Angle 1 is what makes it *specifically Matt's* — a book nobody else in the country is positioned to write. That's the unfair advantage.

### The one-sentence pitch (if Option 2 + Angles 1 & 2)

> A former pastor on how AI amplified his mania and then helped him survive the depression that followed — and what that means for faith, machines, and being known.

That's a book a New York editor can pitch to their colleagues in one sentence. That matters for acquisition more than any single chapter does.

### What this doesn't mean

- Not a mandate to pivot today. Read the options. Sit with them.
- Not a reason to stop writing journal pieces. The pieces feed every option.
- Not a claim that the strongest market angle is the truest book. Sometimes they're the same, sometimes they're not. If they diverge for Matt, the truer book wins. Market is a consideration; it is not the author.
