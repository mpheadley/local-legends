Indigo Ridge Farms · White Plains

The People Who Came Back

Cale and Kelly Crosby left for the Colorado high country, then came home to the foothills to keep a covenant with nine hundred feet of Alabama dirt.
~640 words · first person

The first time I drove out to Merrill Harper Road, I still had a farm of my own.

That matters to how I saw the place. I was raising flowers on nine acres near the Chief Ladiga Trail, and I was not sure it was going to work. So I turned up the road into White Plains, and Indigo Ridge opened up in front of me. The pasture folding back into the foothills. The chickens moving across ground the cattle had just left. I was not looking at it the way a customer looks at it. I was looking at it the way you look at someone doing the thing you are trying to do, to see if they know something you don't.

Cale and Kelly Crosby raise pasture-raised chicken and pork out there, grass-fed and grass-finished beef, rabbit, eggs, honey, whatever the season hands them. They registered the farm in the fall of 2022. They are certified grass-fed by the American Grassfed Association, they belong to Sweet Grown Alabama, and on market mornings they haul their meat two hours to Pepper Place in Birmingham. That is the farm. It is not the reason I remember the visit.

Kelly did not grow up putting up hay. Before Indigo Ridge she was a family nurse practitioner in the mountains of Colorado, a long way from Calhoun County. They had a whole chapter out West. And then they came back. They bought ground in the foothills on purpose, with their kids underfoot, and started raising animals the way they believe they are owed. The people who take a specific piece of dirt most seriously are very often the ones who left, learned something, and came home to it. I have come to think the coming back is not a footnote to the theology. It is the theology. You cannot keep covenant with a place you are only passing through.

Because that is the word they use. Not "sustainable." Not "clean." The Crosbys will tell you, if you ask, that they were put here to be good stewards of both the land and the livestock. That the ground is a trust and the animals are a charge and farming is a form of obedience. Most people selling you grass-fed beef are selling you a health outcome or a flavor. The Crosbys are selling you those too, but they lead with a doctrine. They believe the land is a covenant.

Blood scene — you write this. The exchange: you and Heather brought them flowers; they sent you home with pork and beef and chicken. You were standing on someone else's working ground while your own was still uncertain, and they encouraged you anyway. Two or three sentences. Don't explain what it meant — report what happened.

I know how their story goes from here, because it crossed mine again. My farm is gone now; I sold it. Theirs is still there, still moving animals across the same ground, still raising kids who will use the word "generations" without thinking about it. You do not raise your infant on dirt you intend to strip.

Ending — you write this. The prayer. On a Tuesday morning this July, Cale and Kelly showed up to a 6:30am prayer for the little church I'm trying to start, and prayed for me. The farmers who believe land is a covenant showed up for a gathering that believes community is one too. Land as covenant, church as covenant — same kind of promise. End on the image, not the sermon.

Otter Creek Farmstead · Ohatchee

What the Tornado Left

A storm took 2,200 hardwoods off Frank and Jane Brown's land. A bird dog named Merlin showed them what was underneath.
~560 words · first person

Every Alabamian of a certain age knows where they were on April 27, 2011.

I know where I was. I know what the sky did. Two hundred and fifty-two people died in this state that day, and for years afterward you could read the outbreak in the treelines. The ragged gaps, the second-growth coming in too even, the places the wind reached down and rewrote. On Frank and Jane Brown's land in Ohatchee, it took 2,200 hardwoods. Then it left them with the question every ruined piece of ground asks: what do you do with what's left.

I have some standing to ask that question. I lost a farm too, not to a tornado, to other weather, and I know the particular grief of standing on land that used to be one thing and isn't anymore. So I am partial to what the Browns did next, which was nothing, for a while. They didn't rush to replant the forest. They let the clearcut sit.

Frank had been hunting quail the old way. Bird dogs, pointers, patience. The kind of hunting that needs a certain kind of landscape. He bought an English Cocker Spaniel from a breeder in North Dakota and named him Merlin. And Merlin changed what Frank saw when he looked at the wreckage. The open ground where 2,200 trees had stood, the disaster, turned out to be exactly what quail need. Edge cover. Openings. The specific architecture of a wingshooting landscape. The storm had accidentally built the thing.

They started rebuilding in 2013. Not the forest. A farm. Otter Creek Farmstead grew up around the land the tornado revealed. A wingshooting lodge, then a breeding kennel for field-bred English Cockers that is now one of the best in the country, then sporting clays and cottages and a grand room where 350 people can stand for a wedding. Jane is an artist, and you can feel it; the place reads like something that grew out of the ground rather than something set down on it. There's a distillery coming. "Fine Alabama Whiskey," they're calling it.

In 2016, Garden & Gun named Otter Creek one of 25 Dream Trips of a Lifetime for the traveling sportsman. That magazine is read by people who have seen most of the good places in the South. To make a list of 25 lifetime destinations is not a regional pat on the back. It is a statement that a piece of land in Ohatchee, the land the storm tried to erase, had become somewhere worth traveling from anywhere to see.

Optional first-person beat — verify. If you've actually been out to Otter Creek, put one real detail here (the kennels, the game room that used to be the family house, the whiskey). If you haven't, leave it reported — don't invent a visit. The honest lens is enough.

I think about that ground the way I've had to think about my own. The tornado did not have the last word. It rarely does. But you have to be willing to stand in the clearing long enough to see what the loss made possible. And you need something, a dog or a season or a stubborn hope, to point you at it.

Redbird Willow Farm · Saks / Anniston

The Farm at the End of the Hard Thing

Michele Hornsby built a place of 120 animals on the exact ground where she lost her son, and turned it into somewhere other grieving people could come and breathe.
~600 words · first person

I want to be careful with this one, because I have been on the other side of it.

I've been the person a family worried about. I made the call to 988 from a room on my own land before things got worse. So when I tell you what Michele Hornsby did with the worst thing that ever happened to her, understand that I am not admiring it from a safe distance. I know a little about what it costs to keep standing on ground that has hurt you.

Michele spent thirty years in sales and marketing. Started as a receptionist, worked every job there was, ended up leading. In 2017 her son Justin died by suicide on the family's property in Saks. He was twenty-six. And the spot where it happened became, in her own words, a place of sorrow and fear. A piece of her own land she now had to decide what to do with.

Her husband Ray built a cross and a bench at the site. They kept going out to it. They had some goats already, for clearing brush. Then a pregnant llama named Justine came to the farm, and Justine delivered her foal near Justin's memorial. On International Bereaved Mothers Day. Michele took it as a sign they were on the right path. They named the foal Willow. Redbird for the long way through grief, Willow for the first thing born there. That is where the farm got its name.

What she built on that ground is almost hard to believe. Redbird Willow is a 501(c)(3) now, out on Lenlock Lane, with more than 120 animals. Llamas and alpacas, miniature horses, Valais Blacknose sheep, Scottish Highland cattle, emus, peacocks, goats you can do yoga with, rabbits, donkeys. She left her career to run it full time. School groups come. Homeschoolers, veterans, recovery programs, nursing homes. The highest point on the place has a thirty-foot cross.

And on the third Thursday of every month, ten or twelve bereaved parents come and sit together. Not a program. Not a curriculum. Just parents who have lost a child, gathering in a place that was built out of exactly that loss. As a pastor I sat with a lot of grief across nineteen years, and I never once found a room that did the work a place like that does. Some griefs don't want to be talked at. They want somewhere to stand, and something warm to put your hand on.

When a Suri alpaca named Chappy was stolen off the farm this spring, Michele didn't talk about him like livestock. "These animals are part of how we help people hold on," she said, "especially in their hardest moments." That sentence tells you what kind of farm it is. Chappy wasn't a head of stock. He was somebody's reason to come back the following week.

Michele has said the whole thing out loud better than I could: "You should run towards the things that are most frightening, to be able to manage them and cope with them." She ran toward the worst ground she owned. And she made it a place people drive to on purpose, to be less alone.

Johnny Ponder · Munford

130 Acres at the Foot of Cheaha

A man spent forty years in the forest, came home, and built a school system around it so the next Munford kids would get what he got.
~520 words · first person

I spend a fair amount of energy, in print and out loud, making fun of people who perform being outside. So let me tell you about a man who just did it.

Johnny Ponder grew up in Munford, at the foot of Cheaha, in the 1950s and '60s. He writes about it now. Short pieces for Alabama Pioneers, and the titles tell you the whole world: boys on bikes, arrowheads in plowed fields, bows cut from creek-side saplings, a grandfather asking why don't you look behind you. A boyhood that lasted maybe twenty years in Alabama's particular geography before it was gone. Then he went into the woods for a living. Forty years a forester. And then he came home.

That's the part I keep turning over. He came home. In 1994 he joined the Talladega County School Board for District 3, the district that holds Munford, the schools he himself walked into as a boy, and he's been there ever since. And in that time he helped build something almost no public school system in Alabama has: a 130-acre outdoor campus at the base of Cheaha, up against the Talladega National Forest.

I don't want to bury the specifics, because the specifics are the point. Twelve outdoor classrooms. An amphitheater. A longleaf pine stand. A low ropes course, a greenhouse, a fish pond, a frog pond, a five-tier wetland, butterfly gardens, a bluebird trail, an aquaculture facility. Inside the buildings: a lobby built to look like a cave, hallways with life-sized trees, a cafeteria painted with the national forest that starts at the property line. Kids in Munford learn biology and ecology by walking into the woods that have been there since before the county had a name.

Ponder's own description of the whole ambition is about as plain as it gets: "We're just trying to encourage kids to get off their laptops and phones and do some stuff outside."

The through line of the man's life isn't hard to find. It's the forest. He grew up in it, worked in it forty years, and came home to build a school around it so the children of Munford could learn what he learned in the same landscape where he learned it. That's not a program a consultant pitched. That's a man who knew exactly what the woods gave him and decided it was worth handing down.

He still writes those little pieces for Alabama Pioneers. Short, specific, plain. About one neighborhood and one decade and one set of boys. They don't argue for anything. They just say what it was. Which is its own kind of forestry, if you think about it: the long memory of a place, kept by someone who was there, set down before the last person who remembers it is gone.
